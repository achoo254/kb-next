> [!TLDR]
> - The "nồm" (damp spell) is a kind of late-winter and early-spring weather in northern Vietnam: the air is very humid and floors and walls get covered in water even though it is not raining.
> - The water appears because water vapour in the air meets a surface colder than the "dew point" and condenses into droplets, like the drops on the outside of a glass of iced water.
> - Drizzle is rain with very small drops, often accompanying humid air at this time; keep the house dry by not opening windows during a damp spell, using a dehumidifier and drying clothes in airy places.

## Introduction

If you have ever lived in Hanoi or another northern province around February to March, you will remember this feeling: there is no heavy rain, yet the floor is slippery as if just mopped, the walls are sweating, clothes hung out never dry, and outside tiny raindrops drift in the air. Northerners call this phenomenon "nồm" (a damp spell). This article explains what it is, why walls "sweat", how drizzle differs from ordinary rain, and a few small things that help keep a home less damp.

## What is a nồm?

"Nồm" was originally the name of the wind blowing from the southeast (the nồm wind), which usually brings moisture in from the sea. In daily life people use the word "nồm" for the whole spell of damp weather lasting through late winter and early spring, when the humidity of the air is very high, often reaching or nearly reaching saturation.

This period usually falls from the end of February to April, just as the northeast monsoon weakens and the more humid air from the sea begins to dominate. If you remember the part about the northeast monsoon, you will see that this is when winter turns into spring: cold spells still arrive, but between them come waves of warm, humid air.

## Why do walls and floors "sweat"?

To understand this we need two concepts: **humidity** and **dew point**.

- **Relative humidity** tells how many percent of the maximum amount of water vapour the air can hold at its current temperature it is actually holding.
- The **dew point** is the temperature at which, if the air cools to it, water vapour starts to condense into droplets. The more humid the air, the closer its dew point is to its real temperature.

Think of a glass of iced water left outside in the heat. After a while drops appear on the outside of the glass. The water has not seeped out from inside: it is water vapour from the air meeting the cold glass, cooling below the dew point and condensing.

What happens in your home is exactly the same. After the cold days of winter, floor tiles, concrete walls and objects in the house are still cold because they hold their temperature for a long time. When a wave of warm, humid air arrives, they have not warmed up yet. Water vapour in the air meets surfaces colder than the dew point and condenses into a thin film of water. That is the water that "sweats" on walls and floors. Glazed tiles, stone and smooth surfaces show it most clearly because the water cannot soak in and so sits as a film.

When the surface temperature rises high enough above the dew point, the phenomenon disappears by itself. That is why a damp spell usually ends when a new cold, dry air mass comes down or when the weather becomes properly sunny and warm.

## What is drizzle?

Drizzle is a type of rain with very small drops, usually under 0.5 mm in diameter, falling slowly and lightly in thin veils rather than in big drops like a shower. Drizzle usually forms from low, thick cloud layers full of water vapour, without strong rising currents like those in thunderclouds.

In northern Vietnam drizzle is common in late winter and early spring, when cold air meets humid air from the sea and creates a low layer of cloud covering the whole sky. Because the droplets are so small, drizzle raises the humidity of the air but the amount of water measured is very little. So on a drizzly day everything feels damp, and clothes hung outdoors hardly dry.

A damp spell and drizzle are not exactly the same thing, but they often go together in the same type of weather: lots of low cloud, high humidity, little sunshine.

## Small things that help keep the house less damp

Since the cause is condensing water vapour, the remedy is to reduce the water vapour in the house or to keep surfaces from being so cold:

- **Close the windows while it is damp outside.** Opening them now only lets in more humid air. Open them when the weather is drier.
- **Use a dehumidifier** or the dehumidifying ("dry") mode of an air conditioner. It makes the air in the room less humid, so water has a harder time condensing.
- **Wipe the floor dry** with a dry cloth or a dry mop, rather than mopping it wet (wet mopping only adds water).
- **Dry clothes in airy places,** using a fan or a dryer if you have one. Hanging them in a damp spot makes them slow to dry and prone to smelling.
- **Keep belongings in airy places, wrapped or raised off the floor,** to protect books, papers and wooden items from damp and mould.
- **Be careful when walking on wet floors.** Glazed tiles with water on them are very slippery, and older people and small children fall easily.

These tips are only ways of looking after your home. Every house ventilates differently, so try them and adjust to suit yours.

## Common mistakes

- **Throwing every window open to "let it breathe" during a damp spell,** which makes the house damper.
- **Mopping with lots of water on a damp day,** which makes the floor slippery and slow to dry.
- **Assuming the wall must be leaking from inside.** Most of the water in a damp spell comes from condensation out of the air; if the wall stays damp for a long time even when the weather is dry, that is when you should suspect a leak.

## Summary

A damp spell happens when warm, humid air flows over surfaces that are still cold, so water vapour condenses into water, just like drops on the outside of a glass of iced water. Drizzle is rain with very small drops from low cloud, often accompanying this period. Understanding the mechanism helps you choose the right response: don't open the windows during a damp spell, keep the air in the house dry and wipe the floor with a dry cloth.
