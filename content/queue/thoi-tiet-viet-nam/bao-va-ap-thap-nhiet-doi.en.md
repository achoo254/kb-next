> [!TLDR]
> - Storms and tropical depressions are tropical cyclones; they form over warm sea water and draw their energy from rising water vapour.
> - In Vietnam a tropical depression has winds of Beaufort force 6–7; from force 8 upward it is called a storm (bão), and the higher the force, the more dangerous.
> - When reading a storm bulletin, look at the position of the centre, the strongest wind force, and the direction and speed of movement, then follow the guidance of the local authorities.

## Introduction

Every year many people in Vietnam follow storm news on TV and phones: "storm No. 3 is currently at…", "winds of force 10, gusting to force 12…". What do these numbers mean, and why do storms form out at sea and then move onto land? This article explains in simple language so you can read a storm bulletin with confidence. The content is general knowledge; when a real storm is approaching, always follow the official announcements of the weather agency and local authorities.

## How do storms form?

Storms and tropical depressions belong to the group of tropical cyclones: a large low-pressure area in which air circulates around a centre. To form, they need a few main conditions:

1. **Warm enough sea water.** The sea surface usually needs to be around 26–27 degrees Celsius or more. Warm water evaporates strongly, and the vapour carries a great deal of heat.
2. **Moist air rising high.** When the vapour rises and condenses into cloud, it releases heat, so the air keeps rising and draws more air in from the surroundings near the surface. This is the storm's "fuel".
3. **The Earth's rotation.** The deflecting force caused by the Earth's rotation (the Coriolis force) makes the air flowing toward the centre spin. In the Northern Hemisphere storms turn counterclockwise. Near the equator this force is too weak, so storms hardly ever form right on the equator.
4. **Upper-level winds that do not change too much with height**, so the vortex is not torn apart.

Once over land or cold water, a storm loses its supply of vapour and weakens, but heavy rain and wind can still cause damage for days after landfall.

### The structure of a storm

In the middle of a strong storm there is usually an **eye**, a fairly calm region with little cloud. Around the eye is the **eyewall**, where the wind is strongest and the rain heaviest. Farther out are spiral cloud bands that stretch for hundreds of kilometres. So it is not only the place the storm passes directly that gets rain and wind; the wide area around it is affected too.

## How is a tropical depression different from a storm?

The two terms differ in wind strength. Under Vietnam's classification, based on the strongest wind force near the centre:

- **Tropical depression:** strongest wind force 6–7, possibly gusting to force 8–9.
- **Storm (bão):** strongest wind force 8 or higher.
- **Strong storm:** force 10–11.
- **Very strong storm:** force 12–15.
- **Super typhoon:** force 16 and above.

A tropical depression can strengthen into a storm if conditions are favourable, and conversely a storm that weakens can be called a tropical depression again. That is why bulletins keep tracking depressions.

## What is a wind force?

Wind force in Vietnam uses the **Beaufort** scale, which divides wind strength into forces from 0 to 12 (and extends higher when talking about very strong storms). Each force corresponds to a range of wind speeds and to signs you can observe. The original scale was proposed by a British naval officer named Francis Beaufort in the early 19th century, so that sailors could estimate wind strength at sea. Two easy marks to remember:

- **Force 8** is wind of roughly 62 to 74 km/h, strong enough to break small branches and make walking outdoors very hard.
- **Force 12** is wind of roughly 118 km/h and above, with enormous destructive power.

Bulletins usually give two numbers: the **strongest wind** (sustained wind) and the **gusts** (sudden winds that are stronger for a short time). For example, "strongest wind force 10, gusting to force 12" means the wind is steadily around force 10 but at times reaches force 12.

## How to read a storm bulletin

A storm bulletin usually contains the following information. Look for each item:

- **The name or number of the storm.** Domestically they are numbered in order within the year, for example "storm No. 1", "storm No. 2". Internationally each storm is given its own name (taken from a list proposed by countries in the region), so a bulletin sometimes gives both.
- **The position of the centre:** longitude and latitude, or a description such as "about Y km east of island X".
- **Intensity:** the strongest wind force and the gust force.
- **Direction and speed of movement**, in km/h.
- **Forecast track:** often shown as a cone representing uncertainty. The cone gets wider the farther ahead it looks, because forecasts get harder the farther out they go.
- **Affected area and warnings:** including wind, heavy rain, high waves, storm surge, floods and landslides.

Keep in mind that a storm does not move along an exactly straight line, and weather agencies update their bulletins at fixed intervals. So read the latest bulletin rather than an old one.

## An example of putting the information together

Suppose a bulletin says: "The storm is about 300 km from the coast of a province, strongest wind force 9, gusting to force 11, moving west-northwest at about 15 km/h." You can estimate that the storm will reach the coast in about 20 hours if it keeps its speed and direction (300 divided by 15). That is only a way to picture it. The official forecast is what you should act on, because a storm can change direction, change speed, and strengthen or weaken.

## Common mistakes when following storm news

- **Looking only at the centre and ignoring the affected area.** Rain and wind can arrive before the centre reaches the coast.
- **Confusing gust force with the steady wind.**
- **Believing rumours on social media.** Prefer official sources, such as the National Center for Hydro-Meteorological Forecasting and local authorities.
- **Thinking the storm has passed when the calm eye arrives.** The eyewall behind it can still bring strong wind.

## Summary

Storms form from warm sea water and water vapour, and they spin because the Earth rotates. A tropical depression is weaker than a storm; the higher the wind force, the greater the destruction. When reading a bulletin, check the position, intensity, direction, speed and the accompanying warnings, then follow official guidance.
