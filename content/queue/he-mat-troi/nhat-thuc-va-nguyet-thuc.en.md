> [!TLDR]
> - A solar eclipse happens when the Moon covers the Sun (at new moon); a lunar eclipse happens when Earth blocks light from reaching the Moon (at full moon).
> - Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to Earth's orbit.
> - Never look at the Sun with the naked eye or ordinary sunglasses; use eclipse glasses that meet ISO 12312-2.

## Introduction

Some mornings the sky suddenly darkens like dusk, the birds stop singing, and then the light returns. That is a total solar eclipse. On another night, the full moon turns orange-red: a lunar eclipse. Both are easy to understand if you have read about the phases of the Moon in the article on the Moon and its phases; here we only need to add the idea of a "shadow".

## How a solar eclipse happens

When the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun, it casts a shadow on part of Earth. People standing in the shadow see the Sun covered. There are three kinds:

- **Total:** the Moon covers the whole disc of the Sun. Only people in a narrow strip see it, and totality lasts at most just over seven minutes, usually much less.
- **Partial:** the Moon covers only part of the Sun; the area that can see it is wider.
- **Annular:** the Moon is farther from Earth than usual, so it looks smaller than the Sun and leaves a ring of light around it.

There is an interesting coincidence: the Sun is about 400 times larger than the Moon but also about 400 times farther away, so the two look almost the same size in the sky.

A solar eclipse can only happen at **new moon**, that is, day 1 of the lunar month.

## How a lunar eclipse happens

A lunar eclipse happens when Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon and the Moon enters Earth's shadow. So it can only happen at **full moon**. When fully inside the shadow, the Moon does not turn black but reddish, because Earth's atmosphere bends and filters sunlight, letting only the red part reach the Moon. The total phase can last up to about 1 hour 47 minutes.

Unlike a solar eclipse, a lunar eclipse can be seen from anywhere on the night side of Earth, and it is completely safe to watch with the naked eye.

## Why there is not an eclipse every month

If the Moon orbited Earth in the same plane as Earth's orbit around the Sun, there would be a solar and a lunar eclipse every month. But the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees. At most new moons the Moon's shadow passes above or below Earth; at most full moons the Moon misses Earth's shadow. Only when the three bodies line up at exactly the right moment is there an eclipse. Each year there are usually about two to five solar eclipses (partial ones included) across the whole of Earth.

## How to watch a solar eclipse safely

Looking straight at the Sun, even when it is almost fully covered, can permanently damage the retina without any pain at the time. Follow these steps:

1. **Use eclipse glasses** that meet the **ISO 12312-2** standard, clearly marked on the glasses. Check that they are not scratched or torn.
2. **Do not use** ordinary sunglasses, X-ray film, welding glass of the wrong grade, or damaged glasses.
3. **Do not look through** a camera, phone, binoculars or telescope unless a dedicated solar filter is fixed in front of the lens. Wearing eclipse glasses in addition does not make these instruments safe.
4. **The easiest way for children:** watch indirectly. Poke a small hole in a piece of card and let the light pass through it onto a white sheet of paper behind; the partly covered Sun appears on the paper. Turn your back to the Sun while doing this.
5. Only during **totality** (when the Sun's disc is completely covered) and only if you are inside the path of totality may you take the glasses off. The moment a little light returns, put them back on. For a partial or annular eclipse, always keep the glasses on.

If your vision is blurry or you see dark spots after watching, see an eye doctor right away.

## Summary

A solar eclipse is the Moon covering the Sun at new moon; a lunar eclipse is Earth blocking the light to the Moon at full moon. They do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted. A lunar eclipse is fine to watch with the naked eye, while for a solar eclipse safety comes first: use ISO 12312-2 glasses or watch indirectly.
