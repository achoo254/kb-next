> [!TLDR]
> - The Moon does not shine by itself; its shape seems to change because we see it from different angles.
> - One cycle of phases lasts about 29.5 days, from one new moon to the next.
> - The lunar calendar month follows this cycle, so the first day is the new moon and the 15th is the full moon.

## Introduction

On the night of the full moon in the eighth lunar month, the whole family sits and admires the round moon; a few weeks later, the sky holds almost only a thin sliver. The Moon is not "bitten off", so what happened? This article explains the phases with a simple picture and shows why the lunar calendar is tied so closely to the Moon.

## What the Moon is

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite. Its diameter is about 3,474 km, roughly a quarter (27%) of Earth's, and it is on average about 384,400 km away from us. Gravity on its surface is only about one sixth of Earth's.

The Moon **does not produce its own light**. We see it because it reflects sunlight. Exactly half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun, just as half of Earth is always in daytime.

The Moon also always keeps one face toward Earth. This is called tidal locking: the time the Moon takes to spin once equals the time it takes to orbit Earth, so we never see the "far side" from the ground.

## Why there are phases

The Moon takes about 27.3 days to orbit Earth once. Meanwhile Earth also moves around the Sun, so it takes longer for the Moon to return to the same phase (for example, from new moon to new moon): about **29.5 days**. This is called the synodic month.

Imagine holding a ball under a lamp placed far away and slowly turning around once. The lamp always lights half of the ball; but depending on where you stand, you see the whole lit part, half of it or only a thin line. The Moon works the same way:

- **New moon:** the Moon lies between Earth and the Sun. The lit half faces away, so we see almost no moon.
- **Waxing crescent:** the lit part starts to grow.
- **First quarter (the first half-moon):** we see half of the disc.
- **Waxing gibbous:** the lit part keeps growing.
- **Full moon:** Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon, and we see the whole lit half.
- After that the lit part shrinks through the reverse phases: waning gibbous, last quarter, waning crescent, and back to the new moon.

The phase also tells you when the Moon rises. The full moon rises as the Sun sets and sets as the Sun rises, so it lights the whole night. The new moon rises with the Sun, so there is almost no moon at night.

## Connection with the lunar calendar

The traditional Vietnamese calendar is lunisolar: each month follows the Moon's cycle, and the year is adjusted to match the Sun. Because the lunar cycle is about 29.5 days, a lunar month has 29 or 30 days.

- **Day 1 (mùng một)** is the new moon, the start of the month.
- **Rằm** (around day 15, usually between day 14 and 16) is the full moon.
- At the end of the month the Moon is only a thin crescent before it darkens again.

That is why Tết, the Lunar New Year, on day 1 always falls on a new moon, and the Mid-Autumn Festival on the 15th of the eighth month always meets a full moon. If you are curious about the folk tales tied to the full moon on that night, read [[The legends of Chú Cuội and Chị Hằng: stories of the moon on the Mid-Autumn night]].

## What else the Moon does for Earth

The Moon's gravity, together with the Sun's, creates the rising and falling tides of the sea. Sea level rises highest around the new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon are almost in a line; sailors call these big tides. This same alignment also causes solar and lunar eclipses, the topic of [[Solar and lunar eclipses: how they happen and how to watch a solar eclipse safely]].

## Summary

The Moon only reflects sunlight, and its phases are just us seeing its lit half from different angles. One lunar cycle lasts about 29.5 days, and the lunar calendar month follows it: day 1 is the new moon, day 15 is the full moon. Next time you look at the sky, you can guess the lunar date just by looking at the moon.
