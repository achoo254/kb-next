## Introduction: Tết is first of all color

As the lunar year draws to a close, many Vietnamese streets suddenly change color: flower markets are packed, kumquat pots heavy with fruit ride on motorbikes, and deep pink peach branches lean against walls. Decorating the home for Tết (the Vietnamese Lunar New Year) is not only about looks. For Vietnamese people, every branch and every sheet of red paper carries a wish for the new year.

This article introduces four familiar things: peach blossoms, yellow apricot blossoms, kumquat trees and red couplets, along with the New Year pole of the past. In this series on Tết, it comes after the house has been cleaned, as described in [[Ông Công, Ông Táo and the 23rd of the twelfth lunar month: sending the kitchen gods to heaven and cleaning the house for Tết]]. Once the house is clean, it is time to give it some spring color.

## Peach blossoms: the pink of the North

Peach blossom is the symbol of spring in northern Vietnam. According to Wikipedia, northerners usually choose red peach to ward off evil and bring good fortune at the start of spring. A familiar explanation is tied to the legend of the peach tree at Sóc Sơn: ghosts fear peach branches because two gods dwell in the tree. This is a folk legend, not a historical event, but it shows why a peach branch is placed where it is easy to see.

When choosing peach, many families pay attention to two things. The first is the shape of the tree: balanced branches with a pleasing form. The second is how far the flowers have opened: with both buds and blossoms, the flowers open gradually during the Tết days and do not fade too early. This is common experience, not a rule, so you can choose by your own judgment.

## Yellow apricot blossoms: the sunshine of the Central and Southern regions

In the Central and Southern regions, yellow apricot blossom (hoa mai) is used more than peach. According to Wikipedia, southerners believe that if the blossoms open exactly at the turn of the year or on the morning of the first day, the year will be lucky and prosperous. So growers often time the stripping of the leaves so that the flowers open at the right moment, and many people wait anxiously to see when their own tree will bloom.

One interesting point: these two flowers roughly divide the country in two, but today both can be seen in many places, because people carry plants to sell and to visit relatives all over the country. The split above is a traditional tendency, not an absolute border.

## Kumquat trees: yellow fruit, green leaves

The kumquat tree is usually placed in the living room. According to Wikipedia, the tree should be lush, with green leaves and yellow fruit, to symbolize abundance. The sight of a whole tree heavy with fruit suggests fullness and plenty, so kumquat is popular even in small homes, because it is easy to keep in a pot.

A practical tip for beginners: choose the plant to fit your space. A small apartment needs only a medium pot of kumquat or a small peach branch in a vase; a very large tree is not necessary. A healthy tree with fresh green leaves, and knowing how to care for it (not letting it dry out, keeping it out of cold drafts), matters more than size.

## Red couplets: writing on red paper

A couplet is a pair of lines of verse that match each other in meaning and wording. According to Wikipedia, Tết couplets are written in Chinese characters on red or pink paper. Today many people also ask a calligrapher, called "ông đồ" (a traditional scholar-calligrapher), to write a character at the start of the year for luck. The image of the scholar sitting on the pavement with ink and red paper has become a cultural scene that many people remember.

If you cannot read Chinese characters, you can still take part: many places sell couplets written in the modern Vietnamese alphabet, or you can ask the writer to explain each line so you understand the wish before hanging it. A good couplet is one that the family understands and likes, not just a sheet of red paper to complete the custom.

## The New Year pole: a decoration of old times

The New Year pole is a bamboo trunk about 5 to 6 meters tall. According to Wikipedia, it is usually raised from the 23rd day of the twelfth lunar month and taken down on the 7th day of the first lunar month. At the top, people hang paper votive items, cactus branches, paper carp and clay chimes. People in the past believed that the pole helped keep away evil spirits and bad omens.

Today the pole is seen less in cities, but it remains in some villages and cultural sites. The period during which it is raised and lowered also shows that the Tết season is quite long, from the end of the twelfth month to the end of the seventh day of the first month.

## Simple decorating tips

You do not need to do everything. A gentle approach for a busy family:

- Choose one main plant that fits your region and taste: peach, apricot or kumquat.
- Place it where it is easy to see, such as the living room or near the ancestral altar, away from direct drafts.
- Add one couplet or a red calligraphy character at the door or on a wall.
- Keep the whole look tidy; decoration is meant to add joy, not work.

## Common mistakes

- Buying the plant too early and leaving it in harsh sun or strong wind, so the flowers drop and the leaves wither before Tết.
- Buying a plant too large for the house, which is both costly and hard to care for.
- Hanging a couplet without understanding its meaning, and sometimes hanging it upside down or choosing one that does not fit.
- Believing that everything must be done; in fact each home has its own habits, and Wikipedia also records different practices by region.

## Summary

Peach, apricot, kumquat and red couplets give the home its Tết color. The North leans toward red peach and kumquat, the Central and Southern regions toward yellow apricot; red couplets and the New Year pole are traditional touches tied to red paper, bamboo and wishes for peace. Choose what suits your home, understand its meaning and keep the atmosphere light. The next article goes into New Year's Eve, the moment of passage between the old year and the new.
