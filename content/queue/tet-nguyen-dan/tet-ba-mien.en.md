## Introduction: one Tết, many personalities

Vietnam stretches from North to South, and the climate and history of each region differ, so Tết carries its own colors in each. This article compares some typical features, following what Wikipedia records. These are general tendencies; each family and locality may still do things differently.

The best-known cake was described in detail in [[Bánh chưng, bánh tét and the five-fruit tray: the essentials on the Tết altar]]. Here we look more broadly: the whole feast, the decorative plants and daily habits.

## The North

According to Wikipedia, a northern Tết feast usually has bánh chưng, jellied pork, pickled scallions and fermented glutinous rice. The five-fruit tray is multicolored and may include bananas, pomelo, persimmon, mandarin, kumquat and pear. The Tết plants are red peach and kumquat. Northerners also have the custom of picking lucky twigs at the start of the year after worship at temples.

Jellied pork is a cold dish that can be prepared many days ahead, so it suits a Tết feast that must last several days. This is the writer's practical remark, not a conclusion of the source.

## The Central region

According to Wikipedia, the Central Tết tray has pickled mixed vegetables, tré (fermented pork rolls), sour pork and galangal roots. In Huế there are sticky rice, roast pork, bitter melon soup and tamarind soaked in sugar; in Hội An there is bánh tổ (a steamed sticky rice cake). The Tết plant is usually yellow apricot, and the Central region does not have the custom of picking lucky twigs at the start of the year.

The Wikipedia page does not give every detail about the five-fruit tray or daily habits of the Central region, so this article does not guess further. If your family comes from here, that is exactly what you can add from your own home customs.

## The South

According to Wikipedia, the South has bánh tét (cylindrical sticky rice cake), watermelon, pork braised in coconut water, stuffed bitter melon soup, nem bì, pickled shallots and rice paper. The five-fruit tray often uses fruits such as soursop, fig, coconut, papaya and mango; this choice is tied to names and meanings that people wish for. Southerners usually avoid fruits whose names sound unlucky, such as banana (which sounds like "falling headfirst") or pomegranate (which sounds like "grenade").

The Tết plant is yellow apricot, and watermelon is displayed too. Wikipedia also records that many families in the Mekong provinces stop cooking for the three days of Tết and use food prepared in advance.

## Why do the differences exist?

Wikipedia gives no single explanation, and this article does not claim a certain cause. A few reasonable points can be seen: each region has its own ingredients and climate, so the foods and plants used at Tết differ; the history of settlement and exchange between regions also left its own habits. For example, bánh chưng and bánh tét are both made from glutinous rice, beans and pork, differing only in shape, and are often mentioned as two symbols of two regions.

What matters for the reader is to see the difference as richness. When a family includes people from several regions, the Tết tray is often where those habits meet and blend.

## An example: the five-fruit tray

The five-fruit tray shows the differences clearly. In the North, the tray is usually multicolored and uses fruits such as banana, pomelo, persimmon, mandarin, kumquat and pear. In the South, according to Wikipedia, people use soursop, fig, coconut, papaya and mango, and avoid fruits with unlucky names. The same offering tray, but the fruit names and the meanings attached to them vary by region.

## Common mistakes

- Believing that the customs of your own region are the only correct ones.
- Over-generalizing: even within one region there are many different villages.
- Joking about someone else's taboos without understanding the context.

## Summary

Tết in the three regions shares the spirit of family reunion but differs in foods, plants and daily habits. The North has bánh chưng, peach and kumquat; the Central region has its own dishes such as tré and pickled mixed vegetables; the South has bánh tét, yellow apricot and pork braised in coconut water. That diversity is exactly the richness of Vietnamese Tết.
