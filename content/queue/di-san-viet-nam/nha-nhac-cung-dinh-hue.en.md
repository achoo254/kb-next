> [!TLDR]
> - Nhã nhạc ("elegant music") is the music of the imperial court, standardised and used in major ceremonies under the Nguyễn dynasty in Huế.
> - UNESCO proclaimed Nhã nhạc of the Huế court a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003, and it was placed on the Representative List in 2008.
> - The ensemble combines stringed instruments, oboes, flutes and drums, and served sacrifices, court audiences and banquets.

## Introduction

If you have ever stood in front of the Imperial City of Huế, you can picture an old ceremony: drums begin, an oboe sounds, and court musicians in formal robes sit on both sides of the courtyard. This music was not just entertainment. It was part of the ritual, helping the ceremony feel solemn and orderly. People call this kind of music **Nhã nhạc**, meaning elegant, proper music.

This article introduces Nhã nhạc in plain language for someone who has never heard it: what it is, when it was played, which instruments it uses and why UNESCO recognised it.

## What is Nhã nhạc?

The term "nhã nhạc" comes from Chinese roots and refers to refined music, as opposed to popular folk music. According to Wikipedia, in Vietnamese the term refers specifically to court music performed from earlier dynasties through to the end of the Nguyễn dynasty. The Nguyễn dynasty (1802–1945) made Nhã nhạc the official court music and standardised it, so "Nhã nhạc of the Huế court" usually points to this period.

One distinction matters: Nhã nhạc is not all of Vietnam's music, and it is not folk music. It belonged to the court, with trained players, rules and links to specific ceremonies. After the Nguyễn dynasty ended, this music lost the support of the court and gradually faded, until it was collected and taught again.

## When was Nhã nhạc played?

Nhã nhạc served the court's ceremonies, for example:

- **Sacrifices** to heaven and earth, ancestors and spirits: the music helped keep the ritual solemn.
- **Court audiences**: on days when the emperor received his officials, and at great occasions such as an enthronement, an ensemble accompanied the ritual.
- **Banquets and receptions**: when the court held a feast or welcomed important guests.
- **Imperial funerals**.

Sources further divide Nhã nhạc into several sub-types by occasion, each with its own name. You do not need to remember them all; what matters is that each ceremony had its own music, ensemble and repertoire. This shows that the court treated music as part of the order of ritual.

Court dance often went with the music. Musicians and dancers wore carefully designed costumes, so a performance was both sound and spectacle.

## Common instruments

Wikipedia lists some instruments commonly used in Nhã nhạc:

- **Kèn bầu**: a conical oboe with a high, carrying sound.
- **Đàn tỳ bà**: a pear-shaped lute with four strings.
- **Đàn nguyệt** (also called đàn kìm): a lute with a round, moon-like body and two strings.
- **Đàn tam**: a three-string lute whose body is covered in snakeskin.
- **Đàn nhị**: a two-string bowed fiddle.
- **Sáo trúc**: a bamboo transverse flute.
- **Drums** played with sticks, plus other percussion.

People in the past often spoke of "bát âm", the eight groups of instruments classified by material, such as metal, stone, silk, bamboo, skin and wood. The idea is that a full ensemble brings together many materials and tone colours to make a harmonious sound.

As for influences, Wikipedia notes that Nhã nhạc was strongly influenced by Chinese court music and later took in some elements from the music of Champa. This is common in court arts: borrow, select, then make it your own.

## Why did UNESCO recognise it?

In 2003 UNESCO proclaimed "Nhã nhạc, Vietnamese court music" (Nhã nhạc of the Huế court) a **Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity**. In 2008 the masterpieces on that list were incorporated into the **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity**, and Nhã nhạc is on it.

"Intangible" heritage means something you cannot pick up like a statue: it is skill, repertoire and manner of performance passed from person to person. If nobody can play any more, the heritage disappears even if the instruments remain in a museum. That is why the listing goes together with safeguarding work: recording, teaching the young and organising performances.

Huế is also home to the **Complex of Huế Monuments**, inscribed on the World Heritage List by UNESCO in 1993. The two complement each other: the palaces are the stage of Nhã nhạc, and Nhã nhạc is the sound of the palaces.

## Hearing it and learning more

Nhã nhạc is still performed in Huế, especially at cultural events. If you visit, look for a performance at the heritage site and notice three things: how the musicians sit in rows, the voice of the kèn bầu and the opening drumbeat, and the court costumes. If you cannot travel, look for recordings on the UNESCO and Wikipedia pages listed in the References.

Nhã nhạc also helps us understand Vietnamese festivals. From palace to village, people used sound to open a ceremony. You can compare it with familiar customs such as New Year's Eve in [[Tết in the three regions, North, Central and South: different foods and habits]]: ceremonies at court and at home differ in scale but share a spirit of solemnity.

## Summary

- Nhã nhạc is court music, standardised and used in ceremonies under the Nguyễn dynasty in Huế.
- The ensemble includes strings, oboes, flutes and drums, often alongside court dance.
- UNESCO proclaimed it in 2003 and placed it on the Representative List in 2008; its value lies in skills that are passed on, not just in instruments.
- To really hear it, watch a performance in Huế or look for an official recording.
