> [!TLDR]
> - Gong culture in the Central Highlands (Tây Nguyên) is a "cultural space": instruments, festivals, players and the places where rituals happen, such as communal houses and water wharves.
> - UNESCO proclaimed it on 25 November 2005 as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.
> - For many communities each gong is tied to a spirit; the sound of gongs links people with the spirit world.

## Introduction

On festival nights in the Central Highlands, when a fire burns in the yard and people gather in a circle, the gongs are the first thing you hear. This is not only "a show for visitors". For many communities the sound of gongs is a way of speaking with ancestors and spirits, and also a way for the whole village to share joy and sorrow.

This article explains why gong culture is called a "cultural space" and why UNESCO recognised it.

## What are gongs?

Cồng and chiêng are percussion instruments made of bronze alloy, round, struck with a mallet or by hand. A familiar way to tell them apart is that a **cồng has a knob** in the middle, while a **chiêng has no knob** and is usually larger and flatter. In everyday speech people call them both "cồng chiêng".

One gong usually produces a single pitch, so people do not play alone but form a **gong ensemble**: each person holds one or several gongs, and together they make a melody. To sound good everyone has to listen to the others and keep a shared rhythm, which is why many people see it as a lesson in community.

## Why a "cultural space"?

UNESCO did not list just the gongs. According to Wikipedia, the "space of gong culture" is made of several components together:

- playing music on gongs;
- festivals and rituals, for example the new rice festival and the water wharf ceremony;
- the places where rituals are held, such as longhouses, communal houses, a water wharf, nearby forests and burial grounds.

In other words, if only a gong hanging in a museum remains, the "cultural space" is gone. This heritage lives when there are players, rituals to play at and places for those rituals. This view helps us see that protecting heritage means protecting the life around it.

## Gongs connect people and spirits

According to Wikipedia, gong culture regards gongs as a special link between people and the supernatural: each gong houses a deity whose power matches the gong's age. In other words, the older the gong, the more it is treasured.

This explains why in many places an old gong is of great spiritual value to a family or clan, not just an object to sell or trade. Major occasions in a person's life and in the year are accompanied by gongs, such as the new rice festival or the water wharf ceremony.

Readers should understand that each ethnic group has its own rites, its own gong pieces and its own understanding. This article only states what is widely recorded; it does not replace listening to local people.

## Which communities keep gongs alive?

Gongs belong to many ethnic groups in the Central Highlands. Wikipedia names some, such as the Ê Đê (Rade), Gia Rai (Jarai), Ba Na (Bahnar), Mạ and Cơ Ho (Koho). Within each group the instruments, pieces and way of holding rituals have their own character. That is why we should say "the gong culture of the Central Highlands peoples" rather than a single rite.

## Challenges today

UNESCO and many sources note that gong culture has been strongly affected by economic and social change: the passing of skills between generations has been interrupted, and in some places gongs no longer carry the spiritual meaning they once did. When young people study or work far away and rarely join traditional rituals, handing on the skill of playing gongs becomes harder.

Today there is the **Gong Culture Festival of the Central Highlands**, held in many provinces of the region, which aims to preserve culture and also attracts tourism. This has two sides: it helps many people learn about gongs and gives more chances to perform, but if it is only "performing for visitors" it can dilute the ritual meaning. So people often stress keeping the rituals, the players and the real living space together.

## How to show respect

If you visit the Central Highlands, a few simple suggestions:

1. Watch an official festival or a performance organised by the community, and ask before filming or taking photos.
2. Do not touch a gong without permission: for many families it is a sacred object.
3. Listen to local people explain the meaning of each gong piece.
4. If you want to support the community, buy handicrafts directly from the people who make them.

## Summary

- Gongs are percussion instruments of bronze alloy, played in ensembles.
- The "space of gong culture" includes players, festivals and the places where rituals happen, so safeguarding it means safeguarding the life around it.
