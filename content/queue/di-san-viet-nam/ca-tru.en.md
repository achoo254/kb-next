## Introduction

There is a kind of Vietnamese singing that surprises many first-time listeners with how little accompaniment it has: one voice, one clapper, one lute and a few drumbeats. This is **ca trù**. It sounds simple, but inside is a very refined art in which every beat of the clapper and every drumbeat has a role.

This article introduces ca trù to newcomers: who performs, which instruments are used, what the lyrics are like and why UNESCO placed it among heritage needing urgent safeguarding.

## What is ca trù?

According to Wikipedia, ca trù (Chinese characters meaning roughly "tally card songs") is also called **hát ả đào**. It is a genre of musical storytelling from northern Vietnam, built around a female voice. One common explanation for the name is that listeners rewarded the singer with tally cards (trù), which then gave the genre its name.

Historically ca trù was tied to many occasions: songs for worship at the village communal house, singing contests, and entertainment for officials and scholars. Wikipedia records that ca trù performers count the age of their profession from the Later Lê dynasty (1428–1789) and that ca trù spread across northern Vietnam from the 15th century; there are also many legends about its origin that we should not treat as certain.

## Three people, three instruments

The most common form of ca trù has three roles:

1. **The singer (ca nương, or đào nương)**: she sings and also beats the **phách**, a pair of bamboo sticks struck on a small bamboo block to keep time.
2. **The lute player (kép)**: plays the **đàn đáy**, a three-string lute that accompanies the voice.
3. **The listener (quan viên)**: takes part by beating the **trống chầu**, the praise drum. Each time the singer reaches a fine passage, the listener strikes one beat; the drumbeat is both praise and a kind of comment on the line.

The interesting part is that the listener does not sit silent. They talk to the singer through the drum, so a session feels more like a conversation than a one-way performance. When you first listen, try noticing where the drum strikes.

## The lyrics: poems and hát nói

Most ca trù lyrics are poems in many forms; **hát nói** is a form that is very popular. The content may praise beautiful scenery, express the feelings of a scholar, speak of love and sadness, or reflect on life. Because the words are poetry, the singer has to understand the language to shape each phrase, and the listener needs a little literary knowledge to appreciate it.

The singing style also differs from many other genres: words are carefully "released", with characteristic ornaments. You do not need musical terms; just listen slowly and notice that each word is sung clearly rather than swallowed.

## A period of decline and the work of preservation

Wikipedia notes that by the late 20th century ca trù had nearly died out: after social upheaval and war this art was not a priority and was associated with negative impressions, so the number of people who could sing or play fell sharply. Later, some artists and researchers began reviving it, collecting repertoire, opening clubs and teaching younger people.

In 2009 UNESCO inscribed **ca trù** on the **List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding**. This does not mean it is "less valuable"; on the contrary, it is precious and fragile, so it needs more effort to protect. As a result many safeguarding activities have followed: festivals, ca trù clubs in many places, and recording and collecting songs.

## Compared with other heritage

Ca trù is an art of small spaces: one room, a few listeners, every note of lute and clapper audible. Gongs, by contrast, belong to a whole community outdoors, and Nhã nhạc is the music of the court. If you want to compare, read [[The gong culture of the Central Highlands: when gongs connect people and spirits]]. These three heritages show that traditional music can differ greatly in rhythm, space and meaning.

## How to get to know ca trù

- Listen to a short hát nói and follow the words, noticing how the phách keeps time.
- Try to find where the praise drum comes in.
- If you are in Hà Nội or another place with a ca trù club, you can attend a performance organised by the club.
- Read more on the UNESCO and Wikipedia pages to learn about the different forms.

## Summary

- Ca trù is a genre of musical storytelling from northern Vietnam, usually with a singer, a lute player and a listener on the praise drum.
- The three main instruments are the phách, the đàn đáy and the trống chầu; the lyrics are usually poems, notably hát nói.
- UNESCO inscribed it in 2009 on the list needing urgent safeguarding, because the art had declined sharply.
