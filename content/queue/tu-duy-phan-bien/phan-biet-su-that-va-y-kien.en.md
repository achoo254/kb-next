> [!TLDR]
> - A fact can be checked against evidence; an opinion is a person's judgment or feeling.
> - Many sentences mix both: separating the checkable part from the judgment makes debates less tense.
> - An opinion is not "wrong" the way a false fact is, but a good opinion still needs reasons and evidence.

## Introduction

When we read the news, sit in a meeting or watch a debate, we often hear sentences like "this restaurant is the best in the city" or "this project is three weeks late". The second can be checked against the work schedule; the first depends on taste. Telling the two kinds of sentence apart helps us ask the right next question, and keeps arguments from drifting off course. This article continues [[Checking the source of a piece of information: five questions before you believe and share]]: once you know the source, the next step is to look closely at what the statement is claiming.

## What are facts and opinions

A **fact** (or factual statement) is a sentence that can, in principle, be checked as true or false through observation, measurement, documents or records. For example: "Hanoi is the capital of Vietnam" or "water boils at 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure". A sentence is still a factual statement even when it is false, such as "Hanoi is the capital of Thailand": we can check it and find that it is wrong.

An **opinion** is a person's judgment, feeling, preference or value: "phở is the best dish", "this city is too noisy". An opinion is not true or false in the checkable sense, but it can have more or less convincing reasons.

There is also the **prediction**: "prices will rise next year". A prediction is about the future, so it cannot be checked right away; we can only look at the reasons and evidence behind it.

## Three ways to tell them apart

### 1. Ask "how would I check this?"

For any statement, ask how you would check it. If there is a clear way (look at figures, read a document, take a measurement), it is a factual statement. If the answer depends on each person's taste or viewpoint, it is an opinion.

### 2. Watch for evaluative words

Words such as "good", "bad", "beautiful", "too", "worth", "should", "terrible" and "wonderful" often signal an opinion. Numbers, dates, proper names and units of measure often signal a factual statement. This is only a hint, though: some people use numbers to hide an opinion, and others use evaluative words to talk about something measurable.

### 3. Split a mixed sentence into parts

Many sentences combine both. For example: "This policy failed because costs doubled." Split it:

- "Costs doubled": a factual statement, which can be checked against figures.
- "This policy failed": a judgment, which depends on what counts as success.

With the sentence split like this, you can agree with the first part and still ask about the second: failed compared with which goal? In this way the conversation moves from "you are wrong, I am right" to "which criteria are we weighing?"

## A short example

A colleague says: "Team B's report is sloppy, it has ten spelling mistakes."

- "It has ten spelling mistakes" is a factual statement: it can be counted.
- "Sloppy" is a judgment: ten mistakes in three pages and ten mistakes in thirty pages might lead us to judge differently.

If you want to give Team B feedback, talking about the number and location of the mistakes will be received better than sticking on the label "sloppy".

## A few notes

- **Opinions are not worthless.** The opinion of an experienced person, with reasons attached, is useful information. What to avoid is treating an opinion as if it were a verified fact.
- **Facts do not say everything by themselves.** Two people can agree on the same figure and still differ on what to do, because they value different things.
- **"Everyone knows" is not evidence.** This phrase often comes right before something that has not been checked.
- **Beware of figures without context.** "Up 50%" sounds large, but going from two to three is different from going from two thousand to three thousand.

## A small exercise

Take a short news item or a post you came across today. Underline once the sentences that are factual statements, underline twice the ones that are opinions, and circle the mixed ones. For each mixed sentence, rewrite it as two separate sentences. After a few rounds of practice you will spot them faster without writing anything down.

## Summary

Facts can be checked, opinions are one person's judgment, and many statements contain both. Asking "how would I check this?", watching for evaluative words and splitting mixed sentences into parts are three simple ways to see clearly what someone is claiming. From there you can discuss more calmly, and you are ready for the next article, about common reasoning errors.
