> [!TLDR]
> - A fallacy is an error in reasoning that makes a conclusion seem reasonable even though the reasons are not solid enough.
> - Five common ones: attacking the person, the straw man, the false dilemma, the hasty generalization, and mistaking "after" for "because of".
> - Spot fallacies to ask for clarity, not to "win" against someone.

## Introduction

In arguments we often hear sentences that sound very convincing but still leave us feeling that something is off. Often the problem lies in the way the reasoning is built, not in the facts. Such errors are called **fallacies**. Knowing their names and shapes helps you pause and ask the right question. This article continues [[Telling fact from opinion: three ways to see what a statement is really claiming]]: once you can separate facts from opinions, we look at how people connect those sentences into an argument.

One important note: pointing out a fallacy does not mean the conclusion is certainly wrong. It only shows that the reasons given are not enough to support the conclusion. The conclusion may still be right for other reasons.

## 1. Attacking the person (ad hominem)

**What it looks like:** instead of discussing the argument, the speaker attacks the opponent as a person.

**Example:** An proposes moving the meeting to the morning, giving the reason that people tend to be tired in the afternoon. Binh replies: "An is often late, so An's opinion is not worth hearing." Whether An is late or not does not make the reason "people are tired in the afternoon" true or false.

**How to respond:** bring the conversation back to the content: "Let's check whether the point about the afternoon is true."

## 2. The straw man

**What it looks like:** distorting or oversimplifying the other person's point into a version that is easy to knock down, then knocking down that version.

**Example:** Lan says: "We should cut down the number of meetings." Minh replies: "So you want to get rid of all meetings, and nobody talks to anyone anymore?" Lan did not say that.

**How to respond:** restate exactly what you said: "I only suggested cutting down, not removing them completely."

## 3. The false dilemma

**What it looks like:** presenting only two options as if no other possibility existed, when in reality there are many directions.

**Example:** "Either we move the whole system to the new tool this week, or we put up with being slow forever." There are plenty of options in between: try it with one small team first, or move over gradually month by month.

**How to respond:** ask "Is there another option between these two?"

## 4. The hasty generalization

**What it looks like:** drawing a conclusion about a whole group from one or two cases.

**Example:** "I ordered from that shop twice and both times the delivery was late, so that shop is always late." Two orders is a very small sample; it may be only a coincidence, or due to when the orders were placed.

**How to respond:** ask "How many cases do we have, and are they representative?"

## 5. Mistaking "after" for "because of" (post hoc)

**What it looks like:** because event B happened after event A, assuming A caused B. The full Latin phrase is *post hoc ergo propter hoc*, meaning "after this, therefore because of this".

**Example:** "Since I switched to my new desk, the team's sales went up, so the new desk raised sales." There may be many other factors, such as the busy season or a new campaign. Two events that follow each other do not necessarily have a causal relationship.

**How to respond:** ask "Did anything else change at the same time? If the desk had not changed, would the result be different?"

## What to do when you spot a fallacy

- **Ask, don't accuse.** "I don't yet see the reason linking these two ideas, could you explain more?" is easier to hear than "You are committing a fallacy."
- **Check yourself first.** We also fall into these errors easily, especially when we are angry or defending something we believe.
- **Don't label mechanically.** Not every sentence that looks similar is a fallacy; someone who mentions the track record of a source may be offering relevant information rather than attacking a person. Look at the specific argument.
- **Practice in a safe place.** Read advertisements, social media comments or debates and try naming the error in your head, rather than in a real quarrel.

## A small exercise

Pick an argument you came across recently. Write down the conclusion, then write down the reasons. Ask: if all these reasons are true, does the conclusion necessarily follow? If not, which link is missing?

## Summary

A fallacy is a reasoning error that makes something false look right. The five common ones are attacking the person, the straw man, the false dilemma, the hasty generalization, and confusing time order with cause and effect. Use them to ask for clarity, not to win. Together with checking sources and telling fact from opinion, this is the foundation trio of everyday critical thinking.
