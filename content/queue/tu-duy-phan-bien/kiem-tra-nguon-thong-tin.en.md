> [!TLDR]
> - Before you believe or share something, ask: who says it, what is the evidence, where did it originate, do other sources confirm it, and when was it posted.
> - A sensational headline or a screenshot is not evidence yet; look for where it came from.
> - If you cannot check it, do not share it: staying quiet for a while is better than spreading something false.

## Goal

After this article you will have a short routine, about five minutes long, for checking a piece of information before you believe it or forward it to others. It works for family group chats, social media posts, news articles and short videos alike.

## Why checking matters

False information is often written to spread easily: it is sensational, it frightens or angers us, and it feels like something "everyone should know". When we read on a phone between other tasks, it is easy to tap share before we have had time to think. Checking a source does not require you to be an expert. It only takes a few well-timed questions and the habit of pausing for a beat before sharing.

## Preparation

- A browser with a search engine.
- Willingness to open a new tab and read "laterally": leave the page you are on and look up what other places say about it.
- A moderate dose of doubt: do not believe at once, but do not dismiss everything either.

## Steps: five questions

### 1. Who is saying it?

Look at the name of the author or the organization that posted it. Are they real, do they have expertise in this topic, and what do they gain if you believe them? A site that does not say who wrote it, and has no About page or contact details, is a warning sign. Someone famous in one field does not automatically have authority in another.

### 2. Where is the evidence?

Does the piece cite figures, documents or studies, or does it only make claims? If it cites a source, open the link and read whether that source really says what is claimed. Many articles quote a study whose content actually says something different, or only part of the story.

### 3. What is the origin?

Much information has been cut, stitched together or taken out of context. A photo may be an old picture from another event. You can use reverse image search (upload the image to your browser's image search) to see where it first appeared. With screenshots, look for the original post instead of trusting the screenshot, because screenshots are very easy to edit.

### 4. What do other sources say?

This is the most effective step: open a new tab and look up the same event in independent sources, such as news outlets with editorial processes, official agencies, professional organizations or encyclopedias. If a big event is reported only by one unfamiliar site, be careful. Fact-checkers recommend this lateral reading because it is faster than trying to judge a page using only that page itself.

### 5. When was it posted?

Check the date. Old news is sometimes reposted as if it were new, and information may have been updated or corrected since. Advice from many years ago may no longer be right.

## A small example

Suppose a message in a family group says that "a certain vegetable cures every disease, and doctors are hiding it". Try the five questions: who sent it and from where (unclear); what is the evidence (no figures); what is the origin (forwarded many times, no author); what do other sources say (health organizations say no such thing); when (no date). The phrase "doctors are hiding it" is a familiar sign of false information, because it both arouses curiosity and makes you distrust people who deserve trust. A reasonable conclusion is not to share it and, if needed, to gently reply to the sender.

## Checking your work

After each check, ask yourself:

- Have I found at least one independent source that confirms it?
- Did I read the original article, not just the headline or a screenshot?
- If this information is wrong, who gets hurt, and can I live with that?

When you can answer all three, you can trust it more, while staying open to changing your mind when new evidence appears.

## Common mistakes

- **Believing it because many people share it.** The number of shares tells you how far something spread, not whether it is true.
- **Reading only the headline.** Headlines are often written to attract clicks, and sometimes the content inside says something different.
- **Looking only for sources that agree with you.** Try to find sources that disagree too; if you only look for what you want to hear, that is selective searching.
- **Believing it because it looks professional.** A beautifully designed site can still be wrong. Judge the content and the source, not the layout.
- **Ignoring your own emotional reaction.** When a piece of news makes you angry or scared right away, that is the moment to slow down.

If you want to test whether you really understand how a source argues, try the technique in [[The Feynman technique: explain it in simple words to see how much you really understand]]: explain the information in your own words. The places where you cannot explain it are often the places you have not checked carefully.

## Summary

Checking a source is a small habit: ask who says it, what the evidence is, where it came from, what other sources say and when it was posted. By spending just a few minutes before sharing, you help make the information around you more trustworthy.
