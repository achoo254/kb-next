> [!TLDR]
> - A good email tells the reader right away who needs to do what, and by when.
> - The subject line and the first sentence decide whether people keep reading.
> - Give each email one main purpose; split different tasks into separate emails.
> - Reread, and check the recipients and attachments before you press send.

## Introduction

Most of us receive many emails a day and have no time to read each one closely. So the reader usually glances at the subject, reads the first few lines, and decides: reply now, leave it for later, or ignore it. An email that is long and rambling easily ends up in one of the last two groups.

The good news is that writing a clear email does not take beautiful prose. It only takes answering three questions first: **what do I want the reader to know, what do I want them to do, and by when do they need to do it?** This article walks through each part of a work email and offers a template you can use right away.

## The subject line: the label on the whole message

The subject is the first thing the reader sees, and sometimes the only thing they see in the inbox list. Think of it as the label on a file box: reading the label tells you what is inside.

A few ways to make a subject clearer:

- Name the specific topic instead of a general one. "October sales report: please confirm the figures" is more useful than "Report".
- If the reader needs to act or there is a deadline, put it briefly in the subject, for example "Approval needed by Friday".
- When you reply in a thread whose topic has changed, change the subject to match so it is easy to find later.

Avoid subjects written in all capitals or with too many exclamation marks. They make the message sound like a demand, even when you do not mean it that way.

## The first sentence: say the most important thing

Many people open with a long paragraph of background and leave the request for the end. A busy reader may stop before reaching it. The opposite approach usually works better: **put the conclusion or the request first and the background after it.**

Compare the two styles:

- Rambling: "As you know, last week our team met about the new quarter's plan, and there were many different opinions about the budget. After putting everything together, I have a few options..."
- Clear: "Nam, I need you to pick one of the two budget options below by 5 p.m. on Thursday so the team can submit the plan in time. Details are as follows..."

The second version tells the reader at once what is being asked and how much time they have. The explanation is still there; it is simply in its proper place.

## The structure of a tidy email

An ordinary work email has these parts:

1. A short **greeting**: "Hi Lan," or "Dear Nam," depending on how formal you need to be.
2. The **purpose** in one or two sentences: why you are writing.
3. The **main content**: the necessary background, information or options. Use short paragraphs, and bullet points when several ideas run in parallel.
4. The **action and deadline**: who does what, and by when.
5. A **closing** and signature: a brief thank-you, your name, your job title, and other ways to reach you if needed.

When several people need to act, write each person's name next to their task, for example "Ha: send the draft by Wednesday. Minh: check the figures by Thursday." If you only write "could everyone take a look", it is very easy for nobody to do it, because everyone assumes someone else will.

## Tone and length

The tone of a work email should be polite but natural. You do not need to write like a formal petition; just use a form of address that suits the workplace culture and the reader. With someone you do not know well, or with a superior, a slightly more formal form of address is the safer choice.

As for length, a simple rule is: the reader should be able to finish it on a phone screen without scrolling too much. If the information is long, put a summary in the message and keep the details in an attachment or a link to a document. Each paragraph should carry one idea.

A few small habits make a message easier to read:

- Use short sentences with a clear subject.
- Avoid abbreviations the reader may not know; spell them out the first time.
- When you give a date, include both the weekday and the date, for example "Thursday, 14 November", to avoid mix-ups.
- Strong emotions such as irritation are often misread in writing because there is no voice or facial expression. If you are annoyed, leave the draft for a while and reread it.

## To, Cc and Reply All

The three address fields have different meanings:

- **To**: the people who need to act or reply.
- **Cc**: people who need to be informed but do not have to do anything.
- **Bcc**: recipients whose addresses are hidden from the others; useful when you write to many people outside the organisation and do not want to expose their list.

Be careful with "Reply All". Use it only when everyone in the thread really needs to read your answer. A "thank you" sent to a hundred people is the fastest way to fill their inboxes.

## Common mistakes

- **Too many tasks in one message.** The reader answers one and forgets the rest. Each main purpose deserves its own email.
- **Forgetting the attachment.** If the message says "attached" or "see the file", check that the file is really attached before sending.
- **Sending to the wrong person.** While you type a name, the mail program may suggest someone with a similar name. Look at the recipients field one last time.
- **No deadline.** "Take a look when you have time" is usually understood as "not urgent", so it often does not get done.
- **Not rereading.** Twenty seconds of rereading catches most typos and sentences with missing meaning.

## Checklist before sending

1. Does the subject name the right topic and, if needed, the deadline?
2. Does the first sentence state the purpose of the message?
3. Are the action, the person responsible and the deadline clear?
4. Are the people in To and Cc the right ones?
5. Is the right file attached?
6. Reread the tone: if I were the reader, would it feel pleasant?

## Summary

A clear work email saves the reader effort: the subject names the right topic, the first sentence states the purpose, and each action comes with a person and a deadline. Start with one small habit, rereading and using the checklist above. Once you are used to writing concisely, the exchanges that follow your emails also get shorter.
