> [!TLDR]
> - An effective meeting starts before the meeting: a clear purpose, an agenda sent early, and the right people invited.
> - During the meeting, the chair keeps time and closes each item: who does what, by when.
> - Short minutes sent the same day help everyone remember what was agreed.
> - If a meeting could be replaced by an email, use the email.

## Introduction

Anyone who has worked for a while has at least one memory of a two-hour meeting after which nobody remembered the result. A meeting is a quick way to discuss and decide something together; but without preparation it is also the quickest way to waste many people's time at once.

This article presents a simple process in three stages: **before the meeting** (purpose and agenda), **during the meeting** (chairing and closing items) and **after the meeting** (minutes and follow-up). It works for small team meetings and for online meetings alike.

## Before the meeting: do you really need one?

Ask yourself before inviting everyone:

- Is the purpose to **announce** one-way information? An email or a shared document is often enough.
- Is the purpose to **discuss, decide or resolve a blocker** that needs several people to speak? That is when to hold a meeting.

If you decide to hold one, settle these points:

1. The **purpose** in one sentence, for example: "Choose the supplier for project X" or "Agree the plan for next week".
2. The **attendees**: invite only those whose input is needed or who must carry out the result. Someone who only needs to know the outcome can receive the minutes.
3. The **duration**: set it by the content, not by habit. A 30-minute meeting often works with more focus than a one-hour meeting stretched out.
4. The **time and place**, or the online meeting link.

## The agenda

An agenda is the list of items to be discussed, usually with their order and expected time. Send it to attendees in advance so they can prepare, for example a day before the meeting. A compact agenda might look like this:

| Time | Item | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| 09:00–09:05 | Purpose and desired outcome | Chair |
| 09:05–09:20 | Current situation and figures | Ha |
| 09:20–09:45 | Discussion of two options | Whole team |
| 09:45–09:55 | Decision and action items | Chair |
| 09:55–10:00 | Summary and next meeting | Note-taker |

A few points when building the agenda:

- Put the most important item early, while everyone is still focused.
- Mark which items need a **decision** and which are only for **information**, so attendees know what to prepare.
- Attach the material to read beforehand, with a short note: "Please read pages 2–3 before the meeting".
- Leave a few minutes at the end for closing actions, because it is the part most easily forgotten.

## During the meeting: chairing and closing

Every meeting should have two roles: a **chair** (keeps the pace and the time, invites each person to speak) and a **note-taker**. One person can do both in a small meeting, but speaking and writing at once is harder.

Some habits keep a meeting on track:

- Start on time and restate the purpose in one sentence.
- When the conversation drifts to another topic, write it on a "to discuss later" list and return to the agenda.
- Invite the quieter people to share their views; do not let one or two people use almost all the time.
- End each item with a closing sentence: what was decided, or what information is still needed and who will find it.
- For online meetings: remind everyone to mute when not speaking, and use the chat to share links.

The most important point is that **every action has an owner and a deadline**. "Need to review the budget" is not yet an action; "Ha sends the updated budget table by Friday" is.

## After the meeting: the minutes

Minutes are a short record of what was discussed and agreed at the meeting. Their purpose is not to record every word anyone said, but to let those present and absent alike understand **what was decided and who does what**.

Concise minutes usually include:

1. The meeting name, date and time, the list of attendees and absentees.
2. The purpose or agenda.
3. A summary of the discussion for each item, a few lines each.
4. The **decisions** that were agreed.
5. The **action list**: the task, the person responsible, the deadline.
6. The expected time of the next meeting, if there is one.

Send the minutes **the same day or the next morning**, while memories are fresh. Invite attendees to reply if anything is wrong. Minutes sent a week late are rarely read.

When deadlines arrive, give the owners a brief reminder. At the start of the next meeting, review the previous action list before discussing new items; this habit makes minutes genuinely useful. The email that carries the minutes should also be clear; the article [[Writing clear work emails: help the reader understand and act on the first read]] has tips on writing subjects and stating actions.

## Common mistakes

- **No purpose.** The meeting turns into a chat and ends with no decision.
- **Too many invitees.** People who are not involved sit and listen, while those who need to speak get diluted.
- **An overloaded agenda.** Ten items in thirty minutes means none gets discussed properly.
- **No note-taker.** Everyone remembers it differently and later argues about what was agreed.
- **Vague closing of actions.** Tasks without a name and a deadline tend to drift.

## Quick checklist

Before: one-sentence purpose, attendees, agenda and materials sent.
During: start on time, a chair and a note-taker, close each item.
After: minutes sent the same day, actions with owners and deadlines, reminders when due.

## Summary

A good meeting has a purpose, an agenda and a chair, and ends with concrete actions that people have taken on. Short minutes sent at the right time are the link between words and action. You do not need to apply everything above at once; start with the two easiest habits: send the agenda before the meeting and send the action list after it.
