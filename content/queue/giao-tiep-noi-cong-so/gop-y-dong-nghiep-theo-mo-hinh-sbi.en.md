> [!TLDR]
> - SBI has three parts: Situation, Behavior, Impact.
> - Describe what can be observed instead of labelling personality, so the listener can take it in more easily.
> - Give feedback privately and promptly, end by listening, then discuss the next step together.
> - The model works for praise as well as for feedback that is harder to hear.

## Introduction

Giving feedback to a colleague is one of the hardest things at work. If you say it too gently, the listener does not notice the problem. If you say it too bluntly, they feel criticised and get defensive. Many people choose silence out of reluctance, and the problem keeps repeating.

The **SBI** model is a popular way to give clear feedback without making it heavy. The name comes from three initials: **Situation**, **Behavior** and **Impact**. The core idea is that you speak only about **what you saw and its consequences**, without guessing intentions and without judging the person.

## The three parts of SBI

### S: Situation

State clearly **when and where** the event happened. This helps the listener recall the exact moment instead of arguing "that never happens". For example: "In Tuesday morning's team meeting about the December plan..." or "Yesterday, when you were presenting to the client...".

Avoid "always" and "never". These words make the listener feel accused and produce counterexamples.

### B: Behavior

Describe **the specific thing the person did or said**, in a way an outside observer could also see, as if a camera had recorded it. Distinguish between observation and judgement:

| Judgement (avoid) | Observation (use) |
|---|---|
| "You are irresponsible." | "The report was sent two days after the deadline and did not include the third-quarter figures." |
| "You are very disrespectful to others." | "You interrupted Ha twice while she was presenting." |
| "You are unprofessional." | "You arrived ten minutes late to the client meeting without telling anyone beforehand." |

A judgement like "irresponsible" is the speaker's interpretation; the listener may disagree and will argue about that very interpretation. A specific behaviour is harder to dispute and easier to change.

### I: Impact

Talk about **the effect of the behaviour** on you, the team, the client or the work. This part helps the listener understand why the matter is important. You can speak about the real effect and about your own feelings: "As a result the team had to redo the summary on Thursday evening" or "I felt worried because the client might think we had not prepared well".

Speak about the impact you truly saw or felt; do not speculate for others ("everyone is annoyed with you" is something you can hardly prove).

## A complete example

Suppose Lan is a team lead, and Minh is a colleague who often sends reports late.

> "Minh, I'd like to talk about the weekly report. **(S)** This week and last week, **(B)** the report reached me on Friday afternoon instead of Thursday morning as agreed. **(I)** That left me very little time to put things together before sending them to the manager, and both times I had to work late. How do you see it? Is there something getting in the way that makes the report late?"

The last question is as important as the three parts before it: it opens a conversation instead of passing a verdict. Perhaps Minh is waiting for figures from another department, and the two of you will find a fix together.

## The process step by step

1. **Prepare.** Write out the three parts S, B, I in a few sentences. Check the B part: is this something observable, or is it my own judgement?
2. **Choose the time and place.** Speak in private; do not give feedback in front of a crowd. The closer to the event the better, but if you are angry, wait until you have calmed down.
3. **Open gently.** For example: "I have something I'd like to talk about; do you have five minutes?"
4. **Say S, B, I.** Keep the tone calm and the sentences short.
5. **Stop and listen.** Ask for their view. They may have information you do not.
6. **Discuss the next step together.** Agree on one small, specific change. If needed, arrange to check back after a while.

## Using SBI for praise

SBI is not only for feedback that is harder to hear. Praise with S, B, I is also more specific than "you did well": "In yesterday afternoon's demo, you paused to explain the terminology to the non-technical client. Because of that, they asked many relevant questions, and the meeting ended earlier than planned." Specific praise lets the receiver know which behaviour to **keep**.

## Common mistakes

- **Turning B into a personality label.** "You are sloppy" is not a behaviour. Describe what specifically happened.
- **Adding "but" after praise.** Putting praise and feedback in the same sentence makes the listener distrust the praise. Separate them into two conversations if you can.
- **Too many issues at once.** Choose one or two of the most important.
- **Talking without listening.** SBI is a way to begin a conversation, not a sentence handed down.
- **Waiting too long.** Feedback about something that happened three months ago rarely makes sense; the listener also struggles to recall the situation.
- **Giving feedback by message on a sensitive matter.** Text is easily read in the wrong tone. If you can, talk in person or call, and send a summary afterwards.

## Summary

SBI helps you say three clear things: when the event happened, what the other person did, and how it affected things. When you avoid labels and always leave room for the listener to speak, feedback becomes a conversation for improving the work together.
