> [!TLDR]
> - The Mid-Autumn night in Vietnam is a festival for children: they carry lanterns, often the star-shaped đèn ông sao, in a procession under the moon.
> - The lion dance is a familiar feature, bringing a lively atmosphere and wishes for good luck.
> - You can make a lantern at home with simple materials, taking care with the light source.

## Introduction

If the stories of Chú Cuội and Chị Hằng, told in the previous article, are the "listening" part of Mid-Autumn, then lanterns and the sound of lion-dance drums are the "seeing" and "running around" part. Vietnamese Wikipedia lists lantern processions, lion processions (múa lân) and trống quân singing among the activities of the festival, and notes that in Vietnam Mid-Autumn has developed into a festival for children. This article covers the two most prominent activities: lantern processions and the lion dance.

## Lanterns on the Mid-Autumn night

According to English Wikipedia, during the festival lanterns of all sizes and shapes are carried or displayed, symbolizing beacons of light toward prosperity and good fortune. The same source says that lanterns were not originally tied to moon worship; today the lantern has become a symbol of the festival itself. Because the history of lanterns is debated, treat this as a custom that has changed over time, not as a fixed rule.

### The star lantern

In Vietnam the lantern children know best is the đèn ông sao, the lantern shaped like a five-pointed star. The frame is usually made of bamboo or thin strips, covered with colourful cellophane paper, with a light source inside. The star lantern is easy to recognise, easy to make and cheap, so it has been the "Mid-Autumn toy" of many generations.

Besides the star lantern, shops sell many other kinds: lanterns shaped like fish, rabbits, houses, cartoon characters, or revolving lanterns with figures that chase each other around. The carp shape is also mentioned by Vietnamese Wikipedia among the lantern-procession activities.

### Making a lantern at home

If you want to make a lantern together with a child, a simple way is:

1. **Prepare**: cardboard or thin bamboo strips, coloured paper or cellophane, glue, scissors, a string or a stick to hold it.
2. **Make the frame** in the shape of a star or a box out of cardboard, and cut windows so that the light can shine through.
3. **Glue coloured paper** over the windows and decorate with hand-drawn patterns.
4. **Attach a string or stick** to carry it.
5. **Put a light source** inside.

On safety: use a small battery-powered LED instead of a candle, because candles easily set paper on fire. If you do use a candle, an adult must be with the children. Adults should cut the parts that need a knife or sharp scissors.

## Lantern processions

A lantern procession is when children carry lanterns and walk in a group, usually in the evening once the moon has risen, through the neighbourhood, a residential area or a schoolyard, sometimes singing Mid-Autumn songs. The nice thing about a procession is that the whole community takes part: many places organise it by neighbourhood, and children meet friends, play together and sing together.

A few notes for organisers or for those walking with a child:

- Choose a route with little traffic, preferably a playground or internal paths.
- Adults should walk along and keep young children in sight.
- Bring a spare flashlight in case it gets dark.
- Remind the children to keep the lantern away from hair and clothes if it has a candle.

English Wikipedia also mentions tò he, Vietnamese figurines moulded from rice dough, another toy associated with the occasion.

## The lion dance

The lion dance is the activity that creates the liveliest atmosphere of the festive night. English Wikipedia describes the lion dance as a traditional dance in Chinese culture and in many East and Southeast Asian countries, usually accompanied by drums, gongs and cymbals, meant to bring good luck. In Vietnam the lion dance also appears at Tết Nguyên Đán (the Lunar New Year) and at shop openings, but Mid-Autumn is the occasion that children love especially.

A lion-dance troupe usually has two people operating the head and body of the lion, plus a drummer. A comic character often accompanies the lion to tease and guide it. Children love to watch the lion "eat" sweets, jump to the drumbeat, and move with the procession from house to house.

If you take small children to watch a lion dance, stand a little way back: the loud drums can frighten very young children, and the troupe needs space to move.

## From lanterns to the feast tray

After the lantern procession and the lion dance, children usually gather to eat cakes, sweets and fruit from the full-moon feast tray. That part, together with the different kinds of mooncakes, is told in [[Mooncakes and phá cỗ: the full-moon feast tray for children]].

## Summary

Star lanterns, lantern processions and the lion dance make up the festive Mid-Autumn night of Vietnamese children. The lantern symbolizes light and good luck, the procession is an activity the whole neighbourhood joins, and the lion dance brings drumbeats and joy. Make a lantern together with your child, take care with the light source, and let the children enjoy the night.
