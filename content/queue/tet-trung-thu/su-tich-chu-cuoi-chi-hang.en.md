> [!TLDR]
> - The Mid-Autumn Festival (Tết Trung thu) falls on the night of the full moon of the eighth lunar month, when the autumn moon is at its roundest and brightest.
> - In Vietnam the dark shape on the moon is linked to Chú Cuội sitting under a banyan tree; Chinese tradition tells of Chang'e (Hằng Nga), and there is also a variant about a cassia tree.
> - Knowing these tales helps children and adults see why the moon sits at the centre of the Mid-Autumn night.

## Introduction

Every year, once autumn has arrived and the moon rises full and round on the fifteenth night of the eighth lunar month, Vietnamese children eagerly wait for Tết Trung thu. The festival is also called the Children's Festival (Tết Thiếu nhi) or the Reunion Festival (Tết Đoàn viên). Because it follows the lunar calendar, its date is not fixed on the solar calendar: according to Wikipedia, it can fall anywhere from early September to early October.

This article retells the moon stories that adults usually tell children on the night of the full moon: Chú Cuội (Uncle Cuội), Chị Hằng (Sister Hằng, the Moon lady) and a few images that go with them. These are legends and folk tales, so every region and every storyteller may tell them a little differently. The aim here is to give you the main plot so that you can retell it to children and grandchildren.

## Why is the moon the main character?

Mid-Autumn is an autumn festival, held at the time of the mid-autumn harvest, when the moon is said to be at its roundest and brightest. According to Wikipedia, many people believe the custom originated in Chinese culture and, over time, developed into a children's festival in Vietnam. The exact origin of the festival is unclear, and sources give different explanations, so when retelling it is better to say "according to legend" than to state it as a historical fact.

When we look at the full moon, the darker patches on its surface form shapes that anyone can imagine things into. Each culture sees a different image, and each image gives rise to different stories.

## The tale of Chú Cuội

In Vietnam the most familiar image is a person sitting under a banyan tree on the moon: that is Chú Cuội. Wikipedia notes that Chú Cuội and Chị Hằng are the two characters that children care about most at Mid-Autumn. The tale goes by several names, including "Sự tích chú Cuội cung trăng" (The legend of Chú Cuội on the moon) and "Cây thuốc cải tử hoàn sinh" (The medicinal tree that brings the dead back to life).

The common plot is as follows (variants may differ in the details):

1. Cuội is a poor man who makes his living cutting firewood. One day he happens to find a precious medicinal tree that can bring the dead back to life.
2. He tells his wife to take great care of the tree and never to water it with dirty water.
3. His wife forgets the warning and waters the tree with dirty water. The tree pulls its roots out of the ground and floats up into the sky.
4. Cuội runs after it, grabs the roots and is carried up to the moon. Ever since, on full-moon nights, people can see the shadow of Chú Cuội sitting under the banyan tree.

Vietnamese folk verse mentions "thằng Cuội ngồi gốc cây đa" (Cuội sitting at the foot of the banyan tree). The poet Tản Đà wrote a poem called "Muốn làm thằng Cuội" (I want to be Cuội), and many songs take Chú Cuội as their subject, for example "Chú Cuội" by Phạm Duy and "Thằng Cuội" by Lê Thương. This shows how deeply the figure of Chú Cuội has entered Vietnamese culture.

One small note: the Cuội of the moon tale is a different character from the "Cuội" in the saying "nói dối như Cuội" (to lie like Cuội), who is also a folk figure. The two stories should not be confused when telling them to children.

## Chị Hằng and the legend of Chang'e

The second character is Chị Hằng, that is, Hằng Nga (Chang'e), the Moon goddess of Chinese mythology. According to Wikipedia, the Mid-Autumn Festival in many East Asian countries is based on the legend of Chang'e. The legend is often told together with Hậu Nghệ (Hou Yi), and it has many variants.

One common version: Hậu Nghệ is a heroic archer and Hằng Nga is his wife. He obtains an elixir of immortality. Hằng Nga drinks it, her body becomes light, and she floats up to the moon and stays there. Because details change from source to source (who obtained the elixir, why she drank it, whether anyone chased her), you can tell it briefly and focus on the image of her living alone in the moon palace.

Another image that goes with Chị Hằng in these stories is the jade rabbit (thỏ ngọc), a rabbit said to pound medicine on the moon. Some traditions instead see a cassia tree in the moon's shadow and, according to Wikipedia, Chinese mythology has a figure named Ngô Cương (Wu Gang) together with a divine cassia tree. Cuội's banyan and the Chinese cassia can therefore be seen as two ways of looking at the same shape on the moon.

## Retelling it to young children

If you want to tell a story on the full-moon night, here are some simple tips:

- **Pick one story** and tell it in a few minutes; do not try to cover every variant.
- **Use the moonlight as a prop**: point out the dark patches on the moon and ask the children what shape they see.
- **Say clearly that it is a story**, so children can tell legend apart from scientific knowledge about the moon.
- **Sing together**: a song about Chú Cuội or the full moon suits the atmosphere of the festive night.

After the stories, the best part of the full-moon night for children is carrying a lantern in a procession under the moon. That part is told in [[Star lanterns, lantern processions and lion dances: the festive night of Mid-Autumn for children]].

## Summary

The night of the eighth full moon is when the autumn moon is at its roundest, and around the moon there are stories that have been told for generations. In Vietnam it is Chú Cuội and his banyan tree; in Chinese legend it is Chang'e, Hậu Nghệ, the jade rabbit and the cassia tree. They are all folk tales with many variants, and their greatest value is that they enrich the Mid-Autumn night with imagination.
