> [!TLDR]
> - Vietnamese mooncakes come in two main kinds: bánh nướng (baked, with a wheat-flour crust) and bánh dẻo (with a roasted glutinous-rice-flour crust).
> - Fillings usually include mung bean, lotus seed or mixed fillings, often with salted egg yolk to balance the sweetness.
> - Phá cỗ ("breaking the feast") is when the family, especially the children, set out the tray of cakes, fruit and sweets and eat together under the moon.

## Introduction

After the lion-dance drums and the lanterns described in the previous article on star lanterns, lantern processions and the lion dance, the Mid-Autumn night usually ends with something very down to earth: the whole family sitting around a feast tray under the moonlight. In the middle of the tray are mooncakes. This article explains the two common kinds in Vietnam, the fillings, and how to set up a tray to "break the feast" (phá cỗ) with young children.

## Bánh nướng and bánh dẻo

According to Wikipedia, Vietnamese mooncakes come in two characteristic kinds: **bánh nướng** (baked mooncake) and **bánh dẻo** (sticky rice mooncake). Both are usually sold seasonally for Mid-Autumn and are often packed in boxes of four.

### Bánh nướng

Bánh nướng has a crust made from wheat flour, cooking oil and a syrup boiled with malt. After the filling is wrapped in, the cake is brushed with egg and baked. The egg wash keeps the crust from drying out and gives it a pleasant smell, which is why the crust has an appealing golden-brown colour. Bánh nướng is usually round or square and small enough to fit in the palm, a few centimetres thick. A mould can print patterns or characters on the top.

### Bánh dẻo

Bánh dẻo has a crust made from roasted glutinous rice flour, syrup and a fragrance such as pomelo-blossom water or vanilla. The crust and filling are pre-cooked; the dough is kneaded, wrapped around the filling and pressed into a mould dusted with a thin layer of flour so that it does not stick. Bánh dẻo needs no baking and can be eaten right away; it is white and soft. According to English Wikipedia, bánh dẻo is not as popular as bánh nướng.

### Fillings

Common fillings include mung bean, red bean, lotus seed, jam, watermelon seeds and mixed fillings (thập cẩm). Wikipedia observes that Vietnamese mooncakes lean toward sweetness, so salted egg yolk is often added to balance it. A mixed filling with salted egg is a familiar choice.

A tip for eating mooncakes with the family: the cakes are rich and quite filling, so cut them into several small wedges so that everyone can taste, and serve them with a pot of tea. If you buy cakes, read the production date and expiry date on the package carefully.

## What is phá cỗ?

Phá cỗ is the folk expression for eating the Mid-Autumn feast tray together. Wikipedia mentions preparing a moon-viewing feast and making full-moon offerings among the activities of the festival. Typically the whole family, or the children of a neighbourhood, set out the tray together, wait for the moon to rise and only then "break" the tray: everyone eats while looking at the moon, telling stories and singing. Because Mid-Autumn is the Children's Festival, children are the main characters of this part.

### What goes on a tray?

A tray can vary from household to household and region to region. Commonly seen items:

- **Mooncakes**: bánh nướng and bánh dẻo.
- **Seasonal fruit**: pomelo, persimmon, grapes, bananas, pears...
- **Sweets, nuts and candied fruit**, and other snacks.
- **Mid-Autumn toys**: star lanterns, masks, tò he.
- **Flowers** and a pot of tea.

A familiar decoration trick is to carve pomelo peel into animals or little lanterns, which children find delightful.

### Steps for a phá cỗ with children

1. **Prepare** cakes, fruit, sweets, a cloth and plates, and arrange them on a tray or a small table in the yard or on the porch.
2. **Set the tray together with the children**: let them arrange the cakes and fruit and place lanterns around it.
3. **Wait for the moon to rise** and let the children gaze at it, recalling the legends of Chú Cuội and Chị Hằng.
4. **Break the feast**: cut the cakes and share them equally so that everyone eats a little.
5. **Clean up** together afterwards.

Notes for an outdoor feast: cover the food to keep insects away, be careful with the knife used to cut the cakes, and keep small children seated near adults.

## Connection to the Lunar New Year

The Mid-Autumn tray and the five-fruit tray at Tết share something: both use cakes and seasonal fruit to express gratitude and the wish for reunion. If you want to read about the essentials of the Tết season, see [[Bánh chưng, bánh tét and the five-fruit tray: the essentials on the Tết altar]].

## Summary

Bánh nướng and bánh dẻo are the two characteristic Vietnamese mooncakes; mung bean, lotus seed, mixed filling and salted egg make up the familiar flavours. Phá cỗ is the moment when the family sets out the tray together, watches the moon and shares cakes and fruit. The tray is small and simple, but preparing it together is what people remember most.
