> [!TLDR]
> - Leaves are green because chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light and reflects the remaining green.
> - When the weather cools and days shorten, chlorophyll breaks down and reveals the carotenoids already present in the leaf (yellow, orange).
> - Reds and purples are usually due to anthocyanins, pigments that many trees make in addition in autumn.

## Introduction

In temperate regions, every autumn whole forests change from green to yellow, orange and bright red. In Vietnam you can also see a few trees, such as the bàng (Indian almond), turn red before dropping their leaves. This is not magic; it is the story of light and the pigments in leaves. This article continues the discussion of colour from [[How a rainbow forms: why it appears opposite the Sun and why you sometimes see a double rainbow]]: this time we ask why an object appears as a particular colour.

## An object's colour is the light it does not "swallow"

When white light falls on an object, the object absorbs some colours and reflects the rest. Our eyes see what is reflected. A leaf absorbs a lot of red and blue light but reflects a lot of green light, so we see a green leaf.

The substances responsible are called **pigments**. Leaves contain three groups of pigments worth remembering: chlorophyll, carotenoids and anthocyanins.

## Chlorophyll: the green pigment that does the photosynthesis

**Chlorophyll** is the main pigment of photosynthesis, the process by which a plant uses light to make sugar from water and carbon dioxide. According to Wikipedia, chlorophyll absorbs strongly in the blue and red parts of the spectrum and weakly in the green, so green is reflected and the leaf looks green.

During the growing season, leaves constantly make and replace chlorophyll, and there is so much of it that it hides the other pigments.

## Carotenoids: yellow and orange already inside the leaf

**Carotenoids** are a group of yellow, orange and orange-red pigments. You also meet them in carrots, ripe chillies and egg yolks. In leaves, carotenoids are present all year, helping to capture light and protecting the leaf, but they are hidden by chlorophyll.

Near the end of the growing season, as days shorten and the weather cools, the tree stops making new chlorophyll and the old chlorophyll gradually breaks down. As the green fades, the yellow and orange of the carotenoids show through. In other words, the yellow of autumn leaves is **not newly made**; it was always there, just not visible until now.

## Anthocyanins: red and purple pigments made fresh

**Anthocyanins** are a group of water-soluble pigments that give red, pink, purple and sometimes blue colours. Unlike carotenoids, according to Wikipedia anthocyanins are often **made anew** in the leaf in autumn in many species, rather than being there from the start. These pigments are also found in berries and red cabbage.

Why would a tree spend effort making more? The explanation is still being researched; some hypotheses suggest that anthocyanins help protect the leaf from strong light while the tree recovers nutrients from it before it falls. Since there is no firm conclusion, we should say only that they probably have a protective role, and not treat the matter as settled.

## Why are some years brilliant and others dull?

The autumn colours of the same forest change from year to year. The factors most often mentioned are:

- **Day length**: shorter days are the signal for the tree to stop making chlorophyll.
- **Temperature**: cool nights favour the making of anthocyanins, but a hard frost can kill leaves before they have time to turn colour well.
- **Daytime light**: sunny days, especially with cool nights, are often linked to brighter reds because the leaves are still making the sugars that feed anthocyanin production.
- **Species**: species able to make anthocyanins give reds, while those with mainly carotenoids turn yellow.

We cannot say for sure whether a particular season will be beautiful; we can only say these are the factors believed to have an effect.

## A small experiment at home

You can observe pigments without complicated equipment. Crush a few green leaves with a little rubbing alcohol in a bowl, filter the liquid, then put a drop on the edge of filter paper or coffee filter paper and let it soak upward. The pigments travel at different speeds, so they sometimes separate into coloured streaks. The result depends on the leaves and the paper, so do not expect every streak to be clear. Note that alcohol is flammable: use only a little and keep it away from flames.

## Summary

- An object's colour is the light its pigments do not absorb; chlorophyll reflects green, so leaves look green.
- When the weather cools and days shorten, chlorophyll breaks down and the yellow and orange carotenoids show through.
- Anthocyanins, often made fresh in autumn, bring red and purple to many species of tree.
