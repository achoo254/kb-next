> [!TLDR]
> - Sunlight looks white but is a mix of many colours; gas molecules in the atmosphere redirect blue light more strongly than red light (Rayleigh scattering).
> - By day we see a blue sky because blue light is spread across the whole sky; at sunset the light crosses a thicker layer of air, so only orange and red remain.
> - The same mechanism helps explain why the Moon turns red during a total lunar eclipse.

## Introduction

Perhaps a child has once asked you, "Why is the sky blue?" or "Why is the Sun red every evening?" The two questions seem different, but they share one answer, which lies in how light collides with very small molecules in the air. This article explains the phenomenon with everyday examples, and no physics background is needed.

## White light is really made of many colours

Sunlight looks almost white. But when it passes through a prism or a drop of water, it splits into a band of colours from red through orange, yellow, green and blue to violet. Each colour corresponds to a different **wavelength**: red has a long wavelength, while blue and violet have shorter ones.

Think of wavelength as the "length of the beat" of light. What matters is that when light meets very small obstacles, light with a short beat (blue, violet) is bent more strongly than light with a long beat (red, orange).

## Rayleigh scattering: the air "spreads" blue light

The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen molecules, which are far smaller than the wavelength of light. When light passes through, these molecules absorb it and re-emit it in all directions. This is called **Rayleigh scattering**, named after the English physicist John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh.

According to Wikipedia, the intensity of light scattered this way is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength. That sounds dry, but the meaning is concrete: blue light (about 450 nm) is scattered roughly five to six times more strongly than red light (about 700 nm). You can check it yourself: (700/450) to the fourth power is about 5.9.

As a result, blue light from the Sun is spread in every direction across the sky, so wherever we look, even away from the Sun, we receive blue light from the air. That is why the daytime sky is blue.

### What about violet?

Violet light has an even shorter wavelength than blue, so it scatters even more strongly. Why isn't the sky violet, then? A few reasons are commonly given: the Sun emits less violet light than blue light, some violet light is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and the human eye is much more sensitive to blue than to violet. Added together, the colour we perceive is sky blue.

## Why is the sunset orange-red?

At midday the Sun is high, and its light passes through a relatively thin layer of air. At sunrise and sunset the Sun is near the horizon, and its light has to travel a much longer path through the atmosphere before reaching our eyes.

Over that long path, most of the blue light is scattered out of the line of sight and goes off in other directions. What remains is light of longer wavelengths: orange and red. So the Sun and the sky around it turn to warm colours. The blue light has not vanished; it is simply reserved for people where the Sun is still high, and where the sky is still blue at that moment.

A small detail: fine dust, smoke and other tiny particles in the air also affect the colour of a sunset, so sunsets on different days can be more or less intense. We should not claim to know the cause of each one; just remember that the main mechanism is the length of the path the light takes through the atmosphere.

## Why are clouds white?

Water droplets in clouds are much larger than gas molecules, a few micrometres or more, so they scatter all colours almost equally through a different mechanism called Mie scattering. When all colours are spread equally, we see white. That is why clouds are usually white or grey, while the empty sky is blue.

## The red Moon during a lunar eclipse

The mechanism of "filtering out blue light" appears in one more place. During a total lunar eclipse the Moon lies in Earth's shadow but is not completely dark: some sunlight passes through the edge of Earth's atmosphere and is bent toward the Moon. Along the way the blue part is scattered out, and only orange-red light reaches the Moon, giving it a coppery red colour. If you would like to understand why this happens and how to watch safely, read [[Solar and lunar eclipses: how they happen and how to watch a solar eclipse safely]].

## Try observing it yourself

- Look at a cloudless sky at midday: the sky is a deeper blue high up and paler near the horizon.
- In the evening, compare the colour of the sky near the setting Sun with the sky on the opposite side.
- When the Sun is near the horizon, do not stare directly at it with the naked eye for long; look at the sky around it to enjoy the colours.

## Summary

- White light contains many colours; gas molecules redirect blue light more strongly than red light (Rayleigh scattering).
- The daytime sky is blue because blue light is spread across the sky; sunsets are orange-red because light passes through a thicker layer of air.
- Clouds are white because large water droplets scatter all colours almost equally, and the Moon is red in a lunar eclipse thanks to orange-red light passing through Earth's atmosphere.
