> [!TLDR]
> - A rainbow appears when sunlight enters a raindrop, is refracted, reflected inside and refracted again, with each colour bent at a slightly different angle.
> - A rainbow is always on the side of the sky opposite the Sun; its centre lies exactly in the direction opposite the Sun as seen by the observer.
> - A double rainbow has a fainter outer arc with the colours reversed, caused by light reflecting twice inside the drop.

## Introduction

After a rain shower, a band of colour across the sky always makes people stop and look. A rainbow is beautiful, but its secret is only light, water and viewing angle. This article explains how it forms step by step, why you only see it when your back is to the Sun and why sometimes there are two rainbows at once. If you have read [[Why the sky is blue and sunsets are orange-red: Rayleigh scattering in everyday life]], you already know that white light contains many colours; here we use that same idea.

## What happens inside one drop

Follow a ray of light as it enters a spherical raindrop:

1. **Refraction on entry.** Light moves from air into water and changes direction. Each colour is bent by a slightly different angle: violet and blue light are bent more than red. This splitting of colours is called **dispersion**.
2. **Reflection inside.** The ray reaches the back of the drop and is reflected.
3. **Refraction on exit.** The ray leaves the drop and is bent once more, so the colours spread further apart.

After these three steps, the light from the drop returns roughly toward the direction it came from, that is, toward a person standing with their back to the Sun. Each drop sends each colour at its own angle; red light is concentrated at about 42° from the direction opposite the Sun, and violet at about 40°.

## Why a rainbow is opposite the Sun

Imagine a straight line from the Sun through your eye, extended behind you and onward in front of you. The point where that line meets the sky is the point opposite the Sun. According to Wikipedia, a rainbow is a circle centred on exactly that point, and the red drops lie on a circle with an angular radius of about 42°.

Interesting consequences:

- You only see a rainbow when the **Sun is behind you** and there is rain or water spray in front of you.
- When the Sun is higher than about 42° above the horizon, the rainbow lies below the horizon, so you can hardly see it from the ground. That is why rainbows usually appear in the early morning or late afternoon.
- A rainbow is really a full circle. From the ground we only see half of it; from an aeroplane or a mountain top you can sometimes see the full circle.
- Each person sees their own rainbow, because the circle is centred on the line through that person's eyes. Two people standing side by side see almost the same rainbow, but in principle it is not the same set of drops.

You can try it: on a sunny afternoon, make a fine spray of water from a small hose in the garden and stand with your back to the Sun. If the angle is right, you will see a miniature band of colour.

## The order of colours and the "seven colours"

In the primary rainbow, **red is on the outer edge** and **violet on the inner edge**. Counting "seven colours" (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet) is only a cultural convention; light actually varies continuously, with no hard boundaries between colours. Wikipedia also notes that the seven-colour convention is linked to Isaac Newton.

## The double rainbow

Sometimes a second, fainter arc appears outside the main rainbow. This is the **secondary rainbow**, caused by some rays reflecting **twice** inside the drop before they leave. Two reflections have these consequences:

- The secondary arc lies at a larger angle, about 50° to 53° from the point opposite the Sun.
- **The colour order is reversed**: red on the inner edge, violet on the outer edge.
- The secondary arc is fainter because some light is lost at every reflection.
- The region between the two arcs often looks darker than the surrounding sky; it is called **Alexander's dark band**, after Alexander of Aphrodisias, who described it very early.

So if you see a double rainbow, you can check it: the inner arc has red on the outside, and the outer arc has red on the inside.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there an end of the rainbow you can reach?** No. A rainbow is not an object sitting in one fixed place. It is light reaching your eye from drops in certain directions, so as you move, the rainbow "moves" with you.

**What about rainbows at night?** Moonlight is sometimes bright enough to produce a faint "moonbow", which often looks almost white because the human eye is less sensitive to colour in dim light. It is much rarer.

## Summary

- A rainbow comes from refraction, internal reflection and refraction again inside a water drop, together with dispersion.
- It is a circle centred on the point opposite the Sun, with an angular radius of about 42° and red on the outside.
- A double rainbow has a fainter secondary arc with reversed colours, thanks to two reflections inside the drop.
