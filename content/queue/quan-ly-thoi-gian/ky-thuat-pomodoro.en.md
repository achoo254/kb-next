> [!TLDR]
> - Pomodoro splits work into 25-minute focused sprints separated by short 5-minute breaks.
> - After four sprints you take a longer break; each sprint is for one task only, without interruptions.
> - It suits tasks that are easy to put off or easy to get distracted from, because starting only requires committing to 25 minutes.

## Goal

This article helps you start using the Pomodoro Technique to sit down to work faster, concentrate better and see how much you really get done each day.

## Introduction: where the name comes from

The Pomodoro Technique was developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. According to Wikipedia, the name comes from the tomato-shaped kitchen timer he used as a university student; *pomodoro* means "tomato" in Italian. Each work sprint is called a "pomodoro".

The idea is simple: instead of sitting for ages with one big task and losing heart, you only commit to concentrating for a short fixed stretch, and then you get a break. That short stretch is much easier to begin than a long work session with no end in sight.

## Preparation

- A timer: a kitchen timer, your phone's timer or a timer app. If you use your phone, turn on do-not-disturb so notifications do not pull you away.
- A sheet of paper or a notes app to list what you need to do today.
- A reasonably quiet place to sit.

## Steps

### Step 1: choose one task

Pick exactly one task for the coming sprint. If the task is too big (for example "write the report"), cut it into a smaller piece (for example "outline the report"). To decide what takes priority, you can use the Eisenhower matrix, sorting by urgent and important.

### Step 2: set the timer for 25 minutes and work

Start a 25-minute timer and do only that task. Do not open other tabs, do not check messages. If a thought pops in midway ("I must message A", "let me look this up"), jot it down on the paper beside you and go back to what you were doing. You will deal with it later.

### Step 3: take a 5-minute break

When the bell rings, stop right away, even if you are in the middle of something. Stand up, drink water, walk around, look into the distance. Avoid things that pull you in for long, such as social media, because a short break easily stretches into half an hour.

### Step 4: repeat, and after four sprints take a long break

After every four sprints, take a longer break, around 15 to 30 minutes, then return for a new cycle. A typical working day may hold from a few up to about ten sprints, depending on the work.

### Step 5: record the sprints you completed

After each sprint, draw a tally mark or an X. At the end of the day, look at the total and at how many sprints each task took. This number helps you estimate better next time.

## Example: two hours of studying

You have one chapter to review in an evening:

1. Sprint 1 (25 minutes): skim the whole chapter, note the headings.
2. Break 5 minutes.
3. Sprint 2 (25 minutes): read the first half carefully, note the main points.
4. Break 5 minutes.
5. Sprint 3 (25 minutes): read the second half carefully, note the main points.
6. Break 5 minutes.
7. Sprint 4 (25 minutes): close the book and answer questions about what you just read.
8. Long break of 15 to 30 minutes.

The self-testing in the last sprint is itself active learning; see [[Active learning and spaced repetition: remember longer with less time]].

## When you are interrupted

In practice, someone sometimes calls or an urgent task cuts in. A simple approach:

- If the interruption can wait, write it down and handle it during a break.
- If it cannot wait, count that sprint as cancelled. Deal with the other task, then start a new sprint from the beginning, rather than trying to "patch" the old one.
- If you finish the task before the 25 minutes are up, use the remaining time to review or revisit it, rather than stopping early.

## Check

After a few days, try to answer:

- Did I complete at least a few sprints each day?
- How many times was I interrupted during sprints, and by what?
- Which tasks usually took more sprints than planned?

If the number of interruptions falls over time, you are training your concentration well.

## Common mistakes

- **Not taking breaks**. If you skip breaks to "save time", you are tired by evening and your efficiency drops.
- **Using breaks to do other work**. A break means leaving the screen and the task.
- **Doing several tasks in one sprint**. One task per sprint; gather small tasks into a sprint of their own.
- **Being rigid about 25 minutes**. The number 25 is a starting suggestion. If your work needs deeper focus, you can try longer sprints; but keep the principle of a fixed time with breaks.
- **Applying it to everything**. For creative work that is "flowing", stopping on the dot can break your momentum; be flexible.

## Summary

Pomodoro is not magic, but it lowers the barrier to starting and teaches you to respect focus time. Today, try exactly one 25-minute sprint on the task you have been putting off, then rest for 5 minutes. If it feels good, add another sprint. And if you want to place the sprints across your whole day, combine it with [[Time blocking: schedule time slots for important work instead of only keeping a list]].
