> [!TLDR]
> - Time blocking means giving each task a specific time slot in the day, like making an appointment with yourself.
> - A to-do list says "what"; a blocked calendar also says "when" and "for how long".
> - Leave gaps for surprises and review your schedule each week so your estimates get closer to reality.

## Goal

After this article, you will know how to turn a long to-do list into a daily schedule with clear time slots, so that important tasks actually get done instead of sinking to the bottom of the list.

## Introduction: why a to-do list is not enough

A to-do list has one weakness: it does not say **when** you will do things. Ten tasks on a list all look as if they can be done "any time", so in the end we usually do the easy ones first, the noisy ones first, and keep postponing the ones that need concentration. A list also hides a simple truth: a day has a finite number of hours. Writing down ten tasks when only six hours are free means some of them will certainly not fit.

**Time blocking** solves both problems. You divide the day into blocks of time, each reserved for one task or one group of tasks. Wikipedia describes it as a time management method in which the day is divided into blocks, and each block is dedicated to a specific task or group of tasks.

## Preparation

- A calendar you open often: a phone calendar, a computer calendar, or a paper notebook with hours marked.
- The list of tasks for the week. If you have not yet worked out which are important, you can use [[The Eisenhower matrix: sorting urgent from important tasks to know what to do first]] before scheduling.
- Five to ten minutes each evening or morning to plan the day.

## Steps

### Step 1: enter the fixed slots first

Put in what is already certain: required work or class hours, meetings, school runs, meals, bedtime. These blocks are the "frame" of the day.

### Step 2: put important tasks in your sharpest hours

Everyone has hours when the mind is clearer, some in the morning, some in the evening. Place tasks that need thinking (writing, planning, studying something hard) in those hours. Light tasks such as answering mail or sorting papers go in when you are more tired.

### Step 3: estimate the time and add a little

How long does each task need? Most of us estimate too short. Add about a third for things you have never done before. Make each block long enough to finish the task, but not so long that the task swells to fit the slot.

### Step 4: batch small tasks into one shared block

Answering mail, short phone calls, paying bills: do not scatter them across the day. Create one or two "errands" blocks (for example 30 minutes at 11 a.m. and 30 minutes at 4 p.m.) and handle them in one run. This also protects you from constant interruptions.

### Step 5: leave gaps

Do not fill every minute. Leave roughly 20 to 30 percent of your time free to catch up on delayed tasks or surprises that cut in. A packed schedule will "break" on the very first morning, and you are likely to give up because it feels like failure.

### Step 6: name each block after a concrete task

Instead of "Work", write "Draft the introduction of the report". A concrete name lets you start the moment the slot arrives, without having to think about what to do.

## Example: a working day

A day for an office worker might look like this:

| Time | Block |
|---|---|
| 07:30–08:00 | Breakfast, review the day's schedule |
| 08:00–10:00 | Most important task: write the report |
| 10:00–10:15 | Short break, walk around |
| 10:15–11:00 | Team meeting |
| 11:00–11:30 | Errands block: mail, messages |
| 12:00–13:00 | Lunch, rest |
| 13:00–15:00 | Second task that needs focus |
| 15:00–15:30 | Buffer gap |
| 15:30–16:30 | One-on-one meetings, discussions |
| 16:30–17:00 | Second errands block, note tasks for tomorrow |

Inside a 120-minute block you can add a technique for splitting time such as Pomodoro: four 25-minute sprints with short breaks.

## Check

At the end of the day, look back at the schedule and ask yourself:

- Which blocks did I finish on time, and which overran?
- Was the most important task done in its block?
- Was the buffer gap used, or was it left over?

Record the difference between estimated and actual time. After a week or two, your estimates will become noticeably more accurate.

## Common mistakes

- **Scheduling too tightly**. Leave gaps as in step 5.
- **Abandoning the whole schedule when one block slips**. Slips are normal; just move that block to the next gap and carry on.
- **Forgetting to schedule breaks and meals**. Rest is also a block; if you drop it, your energy sinks through the day.
- **Blocking time for every tiny task**. Block only the important and time-consuming tasks; batch the small ones.
- **Not looking at the schedule again**. A schedule is useful only if you look at it each morning and adjust it each week.

## Summary

Time blocking turns intention into commitment: important tasks get a time, a length and a place in the day. Try it for a week with two important blocks a day, then adjust to reality. You do not need a perfect schedule, only one realistic enough for you to follow.
