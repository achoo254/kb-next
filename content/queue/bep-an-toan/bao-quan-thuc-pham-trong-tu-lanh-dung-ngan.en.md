> [!TLDR]
> - Keep the fridge at 4 °C or below and the freezer at −18 °C; put a small thermometer inside to be sure.
> - Stack by the rule "cooked on top, raw at the bottom": raw meat and fish go on the lowest shelf, in a covered container, so juices cannot drip onto other food.
> - Don't overfill the fridge, don't leave cooked food out for more than two hours, and label leftovers with the date.

## Introduction

Many households put food in the fridge by the rule "wherever there's space". The fridge stays cold and the food stays inside, but vegetables wilt quickly, yoghurt turns watery, and sometimes liquid from a raw piece of meat drips onto a plate of cooked food. A fridge does not "kill" bacteria; it only slows them down. That is why temperature and arrangement decide how safe your food is and how long it stays fresh.

This article explains why 4 °C is the number to remember, which shelf suits which kind of food, and a few small habits that keep the fridge tidy and safe.

## Why temperature matters

The bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply fastest between a few degrees Celsius and about 60 °C, often called the "danger zone". The World Health Organization (WHO), in its "Five keys to safer food", advises keeping perishable food below 5 °C or above 60 °C. Many other food safety agencies use a rounder figure: 4 °C or below for the fridge and −18 °C or below for the freezer. Using 4 °C is simple and still within WHO's advice.

At freezer temperatures bacteria almost stop multiplying, but they are not destroyed: when the food thaws, they become active again. This is covered in more detail in [[Thawing food safely: three methods to use and one to avoid]].

### Check the real temperature of your fridge

The dial on many fridges is only a "low – medium – high" setting, not degrees Celsius. The surest way is to buy a small fridge thermometer, place it in the middle of the main compartment, wait a few hours and read it. If it is above 4 °C, turn the setting one notch colder and measure again a day later. If vegetables freeze at the edges, the fridge is too cold and you should adjust it the other way.

## One job for each shelf

The cold air in a fridge is not even: it is warmer near the door and colder near the back wall and on the lower shelves. Make use of that.

### The top and middle shelves

This is the place for food you eat without further cooking: cooked leftovers, yoghurt, cheese, portioned meals, cakes. The temperature here is fairly stable and easy to see, so you forget leftovers less often.

### The lowest shelf

Raw meat, fish and seafood go on the lowest shelf, in a sealed container or on a tray with edges. The reason is safety, not just tidiness: juice from raw meat can carry bacteria such as Salmonella, and if it drips onto raw food like salad vegetables, there is no cooking step left to kill them. On the lowest shelf, any leak simply lands on the bottom of the fridge, where it is easy to wipe up.

### The vegetable drawer

The drawer usually has higher humidity, which suits leafy greens and root vegetables. Keep vegetables in a paper bag or in a container lined with paper towel to reduce condensation. Don't wash vegetables as soon as you buy them and put them away wet; standing water makes them rot faster. Wash them just before use.

### The door

The door is the warmest place because it is opened and closed all the time. Keep less sensitive items there, such as dipping sauces, jam, drinks and peanut butter. Fresh milk and eggs belong in the body of the fridge, where the temperature is steadier, rather than on the door.

## Small habits that make the fridge safer

### Cooked food does not stay out for more than two hours

A general rule given by WHO and many agencies: do not leave cooked food at room temperature for more than about two hours. In hot weather this time is shorter. A pot of hot soup can be divided into small, shallow containers so it cools quickly before going into the fridge; a large pot put straight in cools very slowly in the middle and also warms the dishes around it.

### Use lidded containers and label them

A sealed container keeps smells from spreading to milk or butter and keeps food from drying out. A strip of paper tape with the cooking date helps you decide quickly. Cooked leftovers should generally be eaten within about 3–4 days; after that, throw them out, and don't "sniff-test" them. Harmful bacteria often do not change the smell or taste of food.

### Don't overfill the fridge

Cold air needs room to circulate. A packed fridge blocks the vents so some spots are not cold enough. On the other hand, an almost empty fridge uses more electricity because warm air rushes in each time you open the door; a few full bottles of water help it hold the cold.

### Put food away quickly after shopping

Chilled and fresh food should go into the fridge soon after you buy it, especially in hot weather. For a long journey home, a small insulated bag is well worth using.

### Wipe the fridge regularly

Leaked liquid, vegetable scraps and milk smears are where bacteria and mould cling. Once a week, go through it, throw out expired items and wipe the shelves with warm water and a little mild soap. This fits the routine in [[A 15-minute daily cleaning schedule: a clean home without losing your whole weekend]]: tie the fridge wipe-down to a fixed day of the week, for example the day before you shop, when the fridge is emptiest.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting raw meat on an upper shelf**: meat juice can drip onto other food. Move it to the lowest shelf.
- **Leaving the door open** while you think about what to cook: each opening lets warm air in. Look first, then open.
- **Sealing hot food and putting it away at once**: steam condenses and the fridge warms up. Let it cool a little first, but not for more than two hours.
- **Relying on smell to tell whether food is still edible**: it is unreliable; rely on dates and temperature.
- **Never measuring the temperature**: if the fridge is only at 8 °C, everything inside spoils faster than you think.

## In short

A fridge slows bacteria down; it does not kill them. Three things to remember: keep it at 4 °C or below, put cooked food on top and raw food at the bottom in sealed containers, and get food into the fridge promptly and date your leftovers. Do these three things and you avoid food poisoning and throw away less spoiled food.
