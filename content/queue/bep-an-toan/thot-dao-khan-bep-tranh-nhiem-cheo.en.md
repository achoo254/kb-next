> [!TLDR]
> - Cross-contamination is when bacteria move from raw food (meat, fish, eggs) to ready-to-eat food (vegetables, fruit, cooked dishes) through hands, knives, boards or cloths.
> - Use separate boards and knives for raw and ready-to-eat food, or wash them thoroughly in between; wash hands for 20 seconds with soap.
> - A damp kitchen cloth is a stubborn home for bacteria: use separate cloths for hands and for surfaces, and launder them often.

## Introduction

You cooked the chicken thoroughly, yet you still get a stomach ache after the meal. A frequently overlooked cause is **cross-contamination**: bacteria move from raw food to a dish that is not cooked again. A typical example: you cut raw chicken on a board, don't wash the board, then use the same board to slice cucumber for a salad. The chicken gets cooked, but the cucumber does not.

This is the second of WHO's "Five keys": separate raw and cooked food. This article helps you separate them in the right places without changing your whole kitchen.

## How cross-contamination happens

Bacteria do not move by themselves; they are carried. The three main "vehicles" in a kitchen are:

1. **Hands**: touching raw meat, then opening the fridge, turning the tap or reaching for the salt.
2. **Utensils**: knives, boards, chopsticks, scissors and plates used for raw meat and then for ready-to-eat food.
3. **Cloths and sponges**: wiping hands, counters and dishes with the same damp cloth.

There are also splashes, such as when you rinse chicken under the tap, and meat juice dripping in the fridge (see the article on storing food in the fridge).

## Cutting boards

### How many boards are enough

The ideal is two boards of different colours or sizes: one for raw food (meat, fish, seafood) and one for ready-to-eat food (vegetables eaten raw, fruit, bread, cooked food). If you have only one board, cut the ready-to-eat food **first**, then the raw food, and wash the board thoroughly right afterwards.

### How to wash a board

Wash with hot water and soap, scrubbing both sides and the edges, then rinse. Plastic can usually go in the dishwasher. Wooden boards are best washed by hand, wiped dry and stood on their edge to drain, because long soaking makes wood crack. Once a board has many deep cuts, bacteria can hide in them; at that point it is time for a new one. You can read more about board materials in the Wikipedia article listed under References.

## Knives and utensils

A knife used for raw meat should be washed before it touches ready-to-eat food, both blade and handle. A simple way is to prepare two knives, or to keep the habit of "wash it after use, don't set it down on the counter". The same applies to plates: **don't use a plate that held raw meat to hold cooked meat**, even if it looks clean. For grilling, prepare two plates from the start: one for raw meat and one for cooked.

## Kitchen cloths

A damp cloth is what bacteria like best: it is moist, warm and has food scraps. One cloth used again and again for hands, counter and dishes becomes the main "spreader".

- Use **three separate kinds**: a hand towel, a surface cloth and a dish cloth. You can tell them apart by colour.
- **Replace every few days**, or at once when one gets raw meat juice on it; launder at a high temperature according to the fabric label.
- Let them dry fully between uses; hang them in an airy place instead of balling them up by the sink.
- When wiping up raw meat juice, use disposable paper towel first, then wipe again with soapy water.
- Rinse dish sponges clean, wring them dry and replace them when they smell or tear.

## Wash hands at the right moments

Wash with soap and clean water for about 20 seconds (as many health agencies recommend), including between the fingers and the backs of the hands. Wash:

- before cooking and between stages;
- after touching raw meat, fish or eggs;
- after taking out the rubbish, blowing your nose or touching pets;
- before eating.

Dry hands with a **clean, dry** towel; wiping them on an apron that has meat juice on it defeats the purpose.

## Counters, sink and fridge

The counter where you put raw meat should be wiped clean with hot soapy water, then wiped again with a clean cloth, right after use. The sink is where meat juice runs, so scrub it, including around the tap. Fridge handles and stove knobs are also places hands that touched meat go to; wipe them at the end of cooking. The details of turning kitchen cleaning into a short daily habit are in [[A 15-minute daily cleaning schedule: a clean home without losing your whole weekend]].

## Common mistakes

- **Using one board for everything** without washing in between.
- **Rinsing raw chicken under the tap**: splashes spread bacteria around the sink. Cooking it thoroughly is enough to kill the bacteria on the meat.
- **Tasting food with a spoon that was used to stir raw food**.
- **Wiping everything with one damp cloth** all week.
- **Just wiping the board with water** and turning it over: use soap and hot water.

## In short

Cross-contamination is a matter of habit, not of expensive equipment. Separate raw and ready-to-eat food with their own boards, knives and plates; wash hands at the right moments; use separate cloths and replace them often. Each small habit blocks one more path for bacteria.
